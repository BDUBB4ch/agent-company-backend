# Scary Witches, Hags and Monsters of Folklore

## Baba Yaga
In the Russian tales collected by Afanasyev in the 1850s, Baba Yaga is an old woman with a bony leg, iron teeth, a nose that touches the ceiling when she lies across her stove, and breasts so long she throws them over her shoulders. She rides the air in a wooden mortar, driving it with the pestle and sweeping away her tracks with a birch broom. Her hut stands in the deep forest on chicken legs and turns its back on whoever comes; the hero must say "Little hut, little hut, turn your back to the forest and your front to me," and it pivots on its bird feet. Round it runs a fence of human bones with a skull on every post, and the eye sockets glow at night. She eats children.

## Black Annis
Black Annis lived in a cave in the Dane Hills just west of Leicester, under an old oak. In 1797 Lieutenant John Heyrick set the oral tale in verse: "Vast talons, foul with human flesh, there grew / In place of hands, and features livid blue / Glar'd in her visage; while the obscene waist / Warm skins of human victims close embraced." Shepherds who lost lambs tracked them to the cave; "infant blood oft stain'd the gory den." The Leicester Chronicle in 1874 recorded what mothers told children who ran on the Dane Hills: she lay in wait there to snatch them to her bower, "where she scratched them to death with her claws, sucked their blood, and hung up their skins out to dry."

## The Witch of Endor
The one séance in the Bible is 1 Samuel 28. King Saul, who has driven every medium out of Israel, gets no answer from God "neither by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets." He disguises himself and goes by night with two men to a woman at Endor who keeps a familiar spirit. "Whom shall I bring up to thee?" "Bring me up Samuel." She sees him first and cries out; asked what she sees, she says, "I see gods ascending out of the earth," then "an old man cometh up; and he is covered with a mantle." Samuel's ghost asks why he has been disturbed and tells Saul that tomorrow "thou and thy sons shall be with me," and Saul falls full length on the floor. The woman kills a fatted calf and bakes unleavened bread and makes him eat, and he goes out into the night. The next day he dies on Mount Gilboa.

## Hecate
By the fifth century BC she is the goddess of the crossroads, the night and sorcery, Trioditis, of the three ways, and Trimorphis, three-formed, carved with three bodies back to back so she can watch every road at once. She carries two torches and a key, is followed by packs of dogs, and her pillars, the Hekataia, stood at crossroads and doorways. On the dark of the moon the Athenians set out her supper, the deipnon, at the crossroads: food left for her and the restless dead, which the poor took after dark.

## The banshee
The bean sidhe, woman of the mound, follows the old Irish families and keens before a death. Ann, Lady Fanshawe, wrote in her memoirs that in 1642, staying at Lady Honor O'Brien's house, she woke at one in the morning to see at the window, floating at the height of her upper-storey room, a woman in white with red hair and "a ghastly complexion," her body like "a thick cloud," who spoke three times in a loud strange voice and vanished with a sigh "more like wind than breath." In the morning her host told her a kinsman had died in the house that night. In other accounts she is a tiny old woman with eyes red from weeping, combing long streaming hair; folklore holds she can only keen while she combs.

## The sluagh
Alexander Carmichael took the account from an old woman in Uist and printed it in Carmina Gadelica in 1900: the sluagh, the host, are the spirits of the dead who cannot enter heaven until their sins are paid, flying "in great clouds, up and down the face of the world like starlings," and returning to the places where they did wrong. They come from the west, so when someone is dying the door and windows on the west side of the house are barred against them.

## The Wild Hunt
The Peterborough Chronicle records it under 1127, after a corrupt abbot was imposed on the monastery: "Then soon afterwards many people saw and heard many hunters hunting. The hunters were big and black and loathsome, and their hounds all black and wide-eyed and loathsome, and they rode on black horses and black goats." The monks heard the horns blowing at night, all through Lent until Easter.

## The dullahan and the death coach
Thomas Crofton Croker's Fairy Legends and Traditions of the South of Ireland (1825 and 1828) grouped the Munster tales of headless riders under the name Dullahan. The rider carries his own head under one arm; the coiste bodhar, the death coach, is an immense black coach with a coffin on top, drawn by headless horses and driven by a dullahan with a long whip; it rumbles to your door, and, Croker says, if you open it a basin of blood is thrown in your face. The whip made from a human spine and the fear of gold are not in Croker, Yeats or Lady Wilde; they are additions of the 1990s and after.

## La Llorona
The Florentine Codex, compiled by Sahagún from Aztec informants in the sixteenth century, lists as the sixth omen before the conquest a woman heard weeping in the streets of Tenochtitlan at night, crying for her children. The legend as told across Mexico and the Southwest has her drown her own children in a river, die, and wander the water's edge in white wailing "Ay, mis hijos"; folklore holds that to hear her is to be marked for misfortune or death, and that she takes children who go near the river after dark.

## The Cailleach
The Hag of Beara in Ireland and Beira, Queen of Winter, in Scotland is the old woman who made the landscape, dropping boulders from her apron as she strode the mountains. She is a veiled crone with one eye, skin blue or deathly white, in some accounts red teeth; she rides the storms, carries a staff that freezes the ground, and fights the spring until, beaten, she throws the staff under a holly where nothing will grow.

## Jenny Greenteeth
In south Lancashire and round Liverpool, Jenny Greenteeth is both the water hag and the duckweed that hides her: a green skin on a pond a child takes for grass. She is green-skinned, with long hair and sharp pointed teeth, and waits under the weed in ponds, canals and gravel pits to grab the ankle of a child or an old person leaning too far, pull them under and eat them. A Staffordshire warning recorded in the nineteenth century: "Jenny wi' green teeth lives i' th' pool; she'll have thee if tha leans too far."

## The Bell Witch
Between 1817 and 1821, according to the account Martin Van Buren Ingram published in 1894, the farm of John Bell at Adams, Tennessee, was tormented by something that began as a dog-like animal in the field, then knocking on the walls, then the sound of rats gnawing the bedposts, then a voice. It slapped the daughter Betsy, pulled her hair, stuck pins in her, and called the father "Old Jack," promising to kill him. John Bell sickened for three years with a twitching face and a swelling tongue and died on 20 December 1820; the family found a vial of dark liquid in the cupboard, gave a drop to the cat, and the cat died, while the voice laughed that it had dosed him. The whole story is legend, written down seventy years late; what is on record is that John Bell farmed at Adams and died in December 1820.

## Familiars and the Devil's mark
In England the witch's power was her familiar, an imp in the shape of a small animal that she fed on her own blood through a teat the Devil had given her. Michael Dalton's Country Justice (1618) told magistrates that "their said familiars hath some bigg, or little teat, borne by the witch upon their body, and in some secret place, where he sucketh them." Elizabeth Clarke of Manningtree named hers to Matthew Hopkins in 1645: Holt, a white kitten; Jarmara, a fat legless spaniel; Vinegar Tom, a long-legged greyhound with an ox's head that turned into a headless four-year-old child; Sack and Sugar, a black rabbit; Newes, a polecat.
