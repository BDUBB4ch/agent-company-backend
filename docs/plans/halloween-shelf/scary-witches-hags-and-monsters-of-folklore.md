# Scary Witches, Hags and Monsters of Folklore

## Baba Yaga
Her first trace in writing is Lomonosov's Russian Grammar of 1755. The tales that fixed her picture were gathered by Alexander Afanasyev between 1855 and 1863. In them she is Baba Yaga kostianaia noga, "bony leg," or Baba Yaga with the iron teeth. She lies stretched across her stove so that her nose touches the ceiling, and in the retellings her breasts are so long she throws them over her shoulders. She rides the air in a wooden mortar, driving it with the pestle and sweeping away her tracks with a birch broom. Her hut stands in the deep forest on chicken legs and turns its back on whoever comes; the hero must say "Little hut, little hut, turn your back to the forest and your front to me," and it pivots on its bird feet. She eats children. In Afanasyev's Vasilisa the Beautiful three riders pass the girl on the forest road, white at dawn, red at sunrise, black at nightfall, "my bright day, my red sun, my dark night," and the hut is ringed by a fence of human bones with a skull on every post; the gateposts are leg bones, the bolts arm bones, the lock a mouth with sharp teeth. When the black rider passes, the eyes of every skull light from inside and the clearing is bright as day. Vasilisa is sent home with a skull on a stick whose eye sockets burn; in the stepmother's house its eyes fix on the stepmother and her daughters and burn them to cinders by morning.

## Black Annis
The earliest record is two title deeds of 13 and 14 May 1764 naming a parcel of Leicester land "Black Anny's Bower Close." Her cave was in the Dane Hills just west of the town, under an old oak. In 1797 Lieutenant John Heyrick set the oral tale in verse: "Vast talons, foul with human flesh, there grew / In place of hands, and features livid blue / Glar'd in her visage; while the obscene waist / Warm skins of human victims close embraced." Shepherds who lost lambs tracked them to the cave; "infant blood oft stain'd the gory den." The Leicester Chronicle in 1874 recorded what mothers told children who ran on the Dane Hills: she lay in wait to snatch them to her bower, "where she scratched them to death with her claws, sucked their blood, and hung up their skins out to dry." Katharine Briggs records that on Easter Monday Leicester ran a drag hunt from Annis's Bower to the Mayor's door behind a dead cat soaked in aniseed, a custom gone by 1800.

## The Witch of Endor
The one seance in the Bible is 1 Samuel 28. King Saul, who has driven every medium out of Israel, gets no answer from God "neither by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets." He disguises himself and goes by night with two men to a woman at Endor who keeps a familiar spirit. "Whom shall I bring up to thee?" "Bring me up Samuel." She sees him first and cries out; asked what she sees, she says, "I see gods ascending out of the earth," then "an old man cometh up; and he is covered with a mantle." Samuel's ghost asks why he has been disturbed and tells Saul that tomorrow "thou and thy sons shall be with me," and Saul falls full length on the floor. The woman kills a fatted calf and bakes unleavened bread and makes him eat, and he goes out into the night. The next day he dies on Mount Gilboa. The Hebrew calls her a baalat ob, mistress of the ob, the spirit or the pit it speaks from; the Greek Septuagint makes her an engastrimythos, a "belly-speaker," which is where the ventriloquist reading comes from. The Church Fathers split: Origen read it at face value, Samuel came up because Scripture says so; Tertullian, in On the Soul chapter 57, held it was a demon in Samuel's shape. Every reader agreed it worked.

## Hecate
Hesiod's Theogony, around 700 BC, says Zeus "honoured her above all" and gave her a share of earth, sea and starry heaven. By the fifth century BC she is goddess of the crossroads, the night and sorcery, Trioditis, of the three ways, and Trimorphis, three-formed, carved with three bodies back to back so she can watch every road at once. She carries two torches and a key, is followed by packs of dogs, and her pillars, the Hekataia, stood at crossroads and house doors. On the dark of the moon, the last day of the Athenian month, each household set out her supper, the deipnon, at the crossroads: cakes, fish, raw eggs, garlic and leeks, the foods of the dead, with the sweepings of the house purification, and dogs, especially puppies, were sacrificed to her there. It was left for her and the restless dead, and the poor took it after dark. Aristophanes joked about it in Plutus in 388 BC: ask Hecate whether it is better to be rich or hungry, and she will tell you the rich send her a dinner every month and the poor snatch it before it is set down. In the Greek Magical Papyri of late antiquity she is drawn with three heads, cow, dog and maiden, and six hands holding torches (PGM IV 2006 to 2125).

## The banshee
The bean sidhe, woman of the mound, follows the old Irish families and keens before a death; the earliest mention is in a Munster chronicle of about 1380. Ann, Lady Fanshawe, wrote in her memoirs that in 1642, staying at Lady Honor O'Brien's house, she woke at one in the morning to see at the window, floating at the height of her upper-storey room, a woman in white with red hair and "a ghastly complexion," her body like "a thick cloud," who spoke three times in a loud strange voice and vanished with a sigh "more like wind than breath." In the morning her host told her a kinsman had died in the house that night. Lady Wilde in 1887 gives her two faces: a lovely girl with long red-gold hair in a scarlet cloak and green kirtle, or a shrouded woman crouched under the trees lamenting with a veiled face. In other accounts she is a tiny old woman with eyes red from weeping, combing long streaming hair; folklore holds she can only keen while she combs. Her Scottish cousin is the bean nighe, seen at night at a ford rinsing the bloodstained shirts of the men about to die.

## The sluagh
Alexander Carmichael took the account from an old woman in Uist and printed it in Carmina Gadelica in 1900: the sluagh, the host, are the spirits of the dead who cannot enter heaven until their sins are paid, flying "in great clouds, up and down the face of the world like starlings," and returning to the places where they did wrong. They come from the west, so when someone is dying the door and windows on the west side of the house are barred against them. The black birds at a deathbed window are a modern embellishment.

## The Wild Hunt
The Peterborough Chronicle records it under 1127, after a corrupt abbot was imposed on the monastery: "Then soon afterwards many people saw and heard many hunters hunting. The hunters were big and black and loathsome, and their hounds all black and wide-eyed and loathsome, and they rode on black horses and black goats." The monks heard the horns blowing at night, all through Lent until Easter. The oldest full account is Orderic Vitalis's, from the priest Walchelin of Bonneval near Lisieux, walking home from a sick call on the night of 1 January 1091, who heard a noise like an army on the move. There came bearers with biers, on each bier a dwarf with a head like a barrel; then women riding on saddles set with burning nails that pierced them at every step; then a host of knights in black. Walchelin knew dead neighbours in every rank and knew it for the familia Herlechini, Herlechin's household. A dead knight seized him with a hand that burned like fire. Orderic says he heard it from Walchelin himself and touched the scar. In Germany Jacob Grimm traced the host's leader to Wodan; in Scandinavia it is Odin's hunt. In Wales the hounds are the Cwn Annwn. In Pwyll, the First Branch of the Mabinogi, the prince comes on a pack feeding on a stag in Glyn Cuch, "their hair was of a brilliant shining white, and their ears were red," and they belong to Arawn, king of Annwn, the otherworld; later lore gives them to Gwyn ap Nudd. Folklore holds their baying is loudest far off and falls to a whisper as they close in, and that to hear them is a death omen. In the north of England the same sky-pack is the Gabriel Hounds.

## The dullahan and the death coach
Thomas Crofton Croker's Fairy Legends and Traditions of the South of Ireland (1825 and 1828) grouped the Munster tales of headless riders under the name Dullahan. In "Hanlon's Mill" a man walking home near Ballyduff in north Cork sees a black coach sweep past without a sound, drawn by six headless black horses and driven by a headless coachman with a long whip. Croker's Cork ballad "The Death Coach" builds the thing out of the dead: the spokes are dead men's thigh bones, "the pole is the spine of the back," and two hollow skulls serve as lamps. Yeats in 1888 sums it up: an immense black coach mounted by a coffin, drawn by headless horses and driven by a dullahan who carries his own head under one arm; it rumbles to your door, and if you open it a basin of blood is thrown in your face. The whip made from a human spine and the fear of gold are not in Croker, Yeats or Lady Wilde; they are additions of the 1990s and after.

## La Llorona
The Florentine Codex, compiled by Sahagun from Aztec informants in the sixteenth century, lists as the sixth omen before the conquest a woman heard weeping in the streets of Tenochtitlan at night, crying "Oh my children" and "where am I to take you." The earliest printed reference folklorists cite is a sonnet by Manuel Carpio, printed in 1883, in which the Llorona is a woman named Rosalia murdered by her husband. The drowning mother first appears in print in Riva Palacio and Peza's Tradiciones y leyendas mexicanas of 1885. The legend as told across Mexico and the Southwest has her drown her own children in a river, die, and wander the water's edge in white wailing "Ay, mis hijos"; folklore holds that to hear her is to be marked for misfortune or death, and that she takes children who go near the river after dark.

## The Cailleach
The Hag of Beara in Ireland narrates the Lament of the Old Woman of Beare, an Irish poem of the ninth or tenth century, mourning the youth she has outlived; the story goes she lived seven lifetimes before she turned to stone, and the Hag's Stone above Coulagh Bay in Cork is said to be her. In Donald Mackenzie's Wonder Tales from Scottish Myth and Legend of 1917 she is Beira, Queen of Winter, a one-eyed giantess with white hair, dark blue skin and rust-coloured teeth. She made the landscape, dropping boulders from her apron as she strode the mountains. She carries a staff that freezes the ground and fights the spring until, beaten, she throws the staff under a holly where nothing will grow. Before winter she washes her great plaid in the Corryvreckan whirlpool between Jura and Scarba; the washing takes three days, its roar is heard twenty miles inland, and when the plaid comes out white the hills are white with snow.

## Jenny Greenteeth
In south Lancashire and round Liverpool, Jenny Greenteeth is both the water hag and the duckweed that hides her: a green skin on a pond a child takes for grass. She first appears in print in 1850. She is green-skinned, with long hair and sharp pointed teeth, and waits under the weed in ponds, canals and gravel pits to grab the ankle of a child or an old person leaning too far, pull them under and eat them. A Staffordshire warning recorded in the nineteenth century: "Jenny wi' green teeth lives i' th' pool; she'll have thee if tha leans too far."

## Peg Powler and the nursery bogies
Peg Powler is the hag of the River Tees. William Henderson's Notes on the Folk-lore of the Northern Counties of 1866 calls her "a sort of Lorelei, with green tresses and an insatiable desire for human life," who lures people under and drowns or devours them; the foam on the upper Tees is "Peg Powler's suds" and the finer froth "Peg Powler's cream." Nelly Longarms is the same hag elsewhere, reaching out of ponds and wells with long sinewy arms; the grindylow of Yorkshire and Lancashire is small, green and scaly, with long wiry arms and sharp teeth. Oldest of them all is Rawhead and Bloody Bones: "Bloodybone" is in print by about 1548, and John Locke complained in 1693 that servants used him to frighten children. In Somerset, in Ruth Tongue's collection of 1965, he lives in the cupboard under the stairs, crouched on a heap of the bones of children who lied or swore.

## The strigoi, the upir and the rusalka
Romania's strigoi are of two kinds: the strigoi vii, living people whose souls leave them at night, and the strigoi morti, the dead who climb out of the grave. In January 2004 at Marotinu de Sus in Dolj county, after a niece said her dead uncle Petre Toma was visiting her at night and making her sick, six of his relatives dug him up, cut out his heart, burned it at a crossroads, stirred the ashes into water and drank it; they got six months in jail for disturbing the dead. The Slavic word is older: a Novgorod priest signed a manuscript "Upir Likhyi," the wicked upir, in 1047, the first time the word appears anywhere. In January 1732 the regimental surgeon Johann Fluckinger opened seventeen graves at Medvedja and signed the report Visum et Repertum: twelve bodies "quite complete and undecayed," the chest cavities full of fresh liquid blood, and Arnold Paole, forty days dead, with blood flowing from his eyes, nose, mouth and ears, who groaned when the stake went in. The rusalka is the dead girl of the water: a drowned virgin, a suicide, an unbaptised child, one of the "unclean dead" never properly mourned, as Dmitry Zelenin's study of 1916 put it. She is pale, with long loose hair, green or fair or brown, in some districts a beautiful naked girl, in others a hideous hairy thing. In Rusalka Week, after Trinity Sunday in early June, the rusalki leave the rivers, swing in the birches and comb their hair, run through the rye, and catch young men to tickle to death or pull under.

## Familiars and the Devil's mark
In England the witch's power was her familiar, an imp in the shape of a small animal that she fed on her own blood through a teat the Devil had given her. The first English witch pamphlet, printed at Chelmsford in 1566, is about one cat. Elizabeth Francis confessed that her grandmother gave her a white-spotted cat called Sathan and taught her "to feed the said cat with bread and milk"; when she wanted a neighbour harmed it asked "a drop of her blood" each time, and once it turned into a toad. She passed it to Agnes Waterhouse for a cake; Agnes was hanged on 29 July 1566. Michael Dalton's Country Justice (1618) told magistrates that "their said familiars hath some bigg, or little teat, borne by the witch upon their body, and in some secret place, where he sucketh them." Elizabeth Clarke of Manningtree, an old woman with one leg, was kept awake night after night by Matthew Hopkins in 1645 until, with Hopkins and the watchers in the room, she called her imps by name and they came, skipping into her lap to kiss her: Holt, a white kitten; Jarmara, a fat legless spaniel; Vinegar Tom, a long-legged greyhound with an ox's head that turned into a headless four-year-old child; Sack and Sugar, a black rabbit; Newes, a polecat. The searchers, Hopkins told the magistrate, had found three teats on her.

## The Bell Witch and the American witches
Between 1817 and 1821, according to the account Martin Van Buren Ingram published in 1894, the farm of John Bell at Adams, Tennessee, was tormented by something that began as a dog-like animal in the field, then knocking on the walls, then the sound of rats gnawing the bedposts, then a voice. It slapped the daughter Betsy, pulled her hair, stuck pins in her, and called the father "Old Jack," promising to kill him. John Bell sickened for three years with a twitching face and a swelling tongue and died on 20 December 1820; the family found a vial of dark liquid in the cupboard, gave a drop to the cat, and the cat died, while the voice laughed that it had dosed him. The earliest printed mention is a page in Goodspeed's History of Tennessee of 1886, which says only that a "remarkable occurrence" at the Bell farm was a voice with "the attributes of a woman." Ingram claimed to print a manuscript, "Our Family Trouble," written by the son Richard Williams Bell in 1846, which no one outside the family has ever seen; the vial, the conversations, the witch's name and Andrew Jackson's visit all rest on Ingram. What is on record is that John Bell farmed at Adams and died in December 1820. Moll Dyer of Leonardtown, Maryland, is the story of an old woman driven from her cabin by townsmen with torches on a freezing night in the 1690s, found days later frozen to a rock with her hand pressed into the stone, cursing the town. No court, church or census record names her. In October 1972 the National Guard hauled the 875-pound rock to the Old Jail in Leonardtown, where it still sits. The Witch of Yazoo, in Yazoo City, Mississippi, the story goes, lured fishermen to her shack and tortured them; chased by the sheriff in 1884 she sank in quicksand, swearing to come back in twenty years and burn the town. On 25 May 1904 a fire destroyed 324 buildings, and the next morning the heavy chain around a grave in Glenwood Cemetery was found broken in two. The witch first appears in print in Willie Morris's Good Old Boy of 1971, and the county's visitors' bureau says the only document ever found shows a woman owned the plot.
