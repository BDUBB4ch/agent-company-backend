# Potions, Spells and Grimoires: What Real Workings Looked Like

## Lead, papyrus and the name of the target
The oldest spells we can hold are curse tablets, defixiones in Latin, katadesmoi in Greek, from "to pierce" and "to bind": thin sheets of lead scratched with tiny letters, rolled, pierced with a nail and dropped into a grave, a well or a spring where the dead and the underworld gods could read them. The formula names the victim and the victim's mother, lists the parts to be bound, tongue, hands, feet, mind, and hands the job to a god or a restless corpse.

Five such tablets, first century BC, have sat at Johns Hopkins University since 1908, rolled together and pierced by one iron nail; the one against Plotius begs "good and beautiful Proserpina" to "tear away from Plotius health, body, complexion, strength, faculties." Britain has yielded about three hundred, two thirds from two springs. Some 130 came out of the spring under the King's Bath at Bath in 1979 and 1980, addressed to Sulis Minerva and nearly all about theft: "Docimedis has lost two gloves. (He asks) that (the person) who has stolen them should lose his minds and his eyes in the temple where (she) appoints"; "May he who carried off Vilbia from me become as liquid as water," followed by ten named suspects. The temple of Mercury at Uley, dug 1977 to 1979, gave about as many, some 45 of them blank, perhaps bought by people who could not write and spoke the curse over the lead.

The recipe books behind them survive on papyrus. PGM IV, the Great Paris Magical Papyrus, is a seventy-two-page codex from fourth-century Thebes, the longest magical text to survive from the ancient world, with more than fifty workings in Greek and Coptic. Lines 296 to 466 give the "wondrous spell for binding a lover": make two figures of wax or clay, one of Ares with a sword and one of a bound woman he threatens with it; push thirteen needles into the woman's body; recite the formula while copying it onto a lead tablet; tie figures and tablet together with thread and bury them in the grave of someone who died violently or before their time, so the restless ghost does the work. The words run: "I adjure all daimons in this place to stand as assistants beside this daimon. Arouse yourself for me, whoever you are, whether male or female... attract her, NN, born of NN." NN is the blank where the user wrote the names.

Hans Dieter Betz's 1986 translation shows how exact the rest is. The "Sword of Dardanos" (PGM IV 1716 to 1870) is a magnetic stone engraved with Aphrodite, Psyche and Eros, consecrated and put under the tongue while the target is named, so that she dreams of you that night. The "love spell of attraction through wakefulness" (PGM IV 2943 to 2966) takes the eyes from a living bat, lets it go, moulds a little dog of unbaked dough or wax and sets the right eye in its right eye and the left in its left, so she cannot sleep until she comes. The "Stele of Jeu" (PGM V 96 to 172) is an exorcism in which the speaker becomes the god: "Subject to me all daimons... I am the headless daimon with my sight in my feet"; Crowley rebuilt it as the Bornless Ritual.

## The Nine Herbs Charm
The Lacnunga, "Remedies", is a tenth-century English medical book (British Library Harley MS 585). Its Nine Herbs Charm is a galdor, a sung spell, over nine plants, mugwort first, each addressed by name: "Remember, Mugwort, what you made known, what you arranged at the Great proclamation. You were called Una, the oldest of herbs." Then the only myth in Old English verse that names the god: a serpent came crawling, it bit a man; then Woden took nine glory-twigs, struck the adder so that it flew into nine pieces. The refrain is a weapon list: "it has power against poison, it has power against infection, it has power against the loathsome foe roving through the land." The instructions after the song are practical: sing the charm three times over each herb and over the apple; pound them to a powder with soap and apple juice; make a paste of water and ashes, boil fennel in it and wash it with a beaten egg for the salve; then sing the charm into the patient's mouth, into both ears and over the wound before laying the salve on. Word and ointment were one treatment.

## Elf-shot, bees and a salve that still kills bacteria
Twelve sung charms survive in Old English, most in the Lacnunga. Wið færstice, "against a sudden stabbing pain," boils feverfew, the red nettle that grows in through a wall, and plantain in butter, then sings: "Loud were they, lo, loud, as they rode over the barrow," the mighty women who hurl spears; "Six smiths sat, they forged slaughter-spears"; "Out, little spear." The pain is a shot, gods' shot, elves' shot or hags' shot, and a knife dipped in the salve and rubbed on the place draws it out. "For a Swarm of Bees," written in the margin of a Bede at Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, has you throw earth under your right foot, then throw soil over the rising swarm singing, "Sit you down, victory-women, sink to earth! Never fly wild to the wood." The Æcerbot cuts four sods from a failed field at night, dresses their roots with oil, honey, yeast and milk, carries them to four masses and returns them before nightfall with "Erce, Erce, Erce, eorþan modor," Erce, mother of earth, a name found nowhere else. Bald's Leechbook (tenth century) gives an eye salve of garlic and cropleac (onion or leek), equal parts wine and ox gall, stood nine nights in a brass vessel, strained and laid on with a feather; in 2015 microbiologists made it exactly as written and it killed MRSA, and skipping any step weakened it.

## Picatrix and the talisman
The Ghayat al-Hakim, "The Aim of the Sage," was written in Muslim Spain in the tenth or eleventh century, now argued to be the work of Maslama al-Qurtubi, who died in 964. In 1256 a translator at the court of Alfonso X of Castile put it into Spanish, and the Latin version that followed carried the name Picatrix. Four books, four hundred pages: the theory of rays descending from the planets, then the engineering. Choose the day and hour of the planet, wait for the Moon in the right mansion, engrave the figure in the right metal and stone, and fumigate it with the right smoke so the planetary spirit comes down into the object. The ring of Mars, a man in armour holding a sword and a severed head, is cut in iron on a Tuesday and promises victory.

The recipes are exact. Against scorpions, cut a scorpion on a yellow sapphire with the Sun in the first degree of Scorpio, set it in a gold ring and stamp it into chewed frankincense when the Moon is in Scorpio. For the seventeenth mansion of the Moon, make a monkey in an iron seal, fumigate it with monkey and mouse hair, wrap it in monkey skin, call on the lord Adrieb and bury it in the house against thieves. For love, with the Moon in Taurus joined to Venus, make two images marked 220 and 284, the amicable numbers, each the sum of the other's divisors, join them "in an embrace" and bury them in one lover's house. Saturn's fumes are "all things that smell bad, asafoetida, gum arabic, bdellium, hemlock," or the head of a black cat burned with opium, storax, saffron, wormwood and colocynth.

## The Sworn Book of Honorius
The oldest European ritual grimoire is the Liber Juratus, the Sworn Book of Honorius, thirteenth or early fourteenth century, surviving in nine medieval manuscripts, the fullest the British Library's Sloane 3854. Its prologue says a council of masters chose Honorius of Thebes, son of Euclid, to compress their seven books into one of 93 chapters. Its great operation is the Visio Dei, seeing God while alive, reached through fasting, confession, prayer, astrological timing and the Sigillum Dei, the Seal of God, drawn on virgin parchment.

## The Key of Solomon
The Clavicula Salomonis is a Renaissance Italian or Latin text of the fourteenth or fifteenth century that claims King Solomon as author. Its structure is the structure of every working after it: days of fasting and washing; a consecrated knife, a white robe, a hazel wand cut in one stroke; a circle traced on the ground with the names of God round its rim; pentacles of each planet drawn on virgin parchment in the planet's hour; then the conjurations, long prayers commanding spirits by name after name, and operations for invisibility, finding thieves and love. Mathers's English version (1889) sets nine days of preparation. The white-hilted knife is forged on the day and hour of Mercury with Mars in Aries or Scorpio, dipped in gosling's blood and pimpernel juice, and engraved; a black-hilted knife and a short lance trace the circles, quenched in magpie blood and the herb mercury; the pentacles are drawn on a Wednesday in the hour of Mercury.

## The Lesser Key and the shape of a conjuration
The Lemegeton is a later compilation in five books; its Goetia catalogues seventy-two spirits with ranks, offices and seals, which Solomon is said to have shut in a brass vessel. In Mathers's translation (published by Crowley, 1904) the circle is nine feet across, the divine names round its rim running from EHYEH to LEVANAH, the Moon; the Triangle of Solomon stands two feet outside it, three feet across, apex toward the spirit's quarter, Anaphaxeton among its names, and the spirit appears in the triangle, never the circle. With hexagram, pentagram, ring and seal the operator bathes and begins: "I do invocate and conjure thee, O Spirit, N.; and being with power armed from the Supreme Majesty, I do strongly command thee, by Beralanensis, Baldachiensis, Paumachia, and Apologiae Sedes." If nothing comes there follow a constraint, curses and a conjuration of the fire; at the end the License to Depart sends it "unto thy proper place; without causing harm or danger unto man or beast." Nothing is improvised; the words are the engine.

## Agrippa and the sigil
Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa's Three Books of Occult Philosophy (Cologne, 1533; English by John French, 1651) is where the planetary magic squares and their seals come from. Book 2 gives the kamea of each planet, "and out of it is drawn the Character of Mars, and of his spirits." Book 3, in the chapters on the characters and seals of spirits, gives the method still used: lay the letters of a spirit's name on the square and join the numbers they fall on with one line, so the name becomes a shape; or, "by the gathering together of letters," collapse a name such as Michael into one glyph. A sigil is a name folded until it cannot be read, only recognised.

## Icelandic staves and the Galdrabók
Iceland drew its spells. The Galdrabók, about 1600, holds 47 spells and staves by four scribes in Latin, runes and staves; one spell calls on Óðinn, Þór, Freyr and Freyja beside Satan and Beelzebub. There are staves against headache, insomnia and distress at sea, to find a thief, to cause fear, to make the eater of marked food break wind without end, and to win a woman: "cut this sign into a piece of cheese and give it to her to eat." Folklore holds that nábrók, necropants, were made by flaying a consenting dead friend from the waist down in one piece, stepping into the skin and tucking a stolen coin and the nábrókarstafur inside, after which it never ran out of money; the Museum of Icelandic Sorcery and Witchcraft at Hólmavík shows a replica. Iceland held about 120 witch trials in the seventeenth century and burned 21 people, 20 of them men, Jón Rögnvaldsson first in 1625.

## Cunning folk
The people who actually sold magic in England from the sixteenth century into the twentieth were cunning folk: a few thousand at any time in the nineteenth century, by Owen Davies's count, working out of cottages in every county. They found lost spoons and strayed cows, named thieves, drew off bewitchment and sold love charms. Neighbours drew a hard line between the cunning man who lifted a curse and the witch who laid one, and only a small fraction were ever charged under the Witchcraft Acts. Written charms were their bread and butter and unbewitching the dear end: a Lincoln wise man charged a guinea in 1838, a Devon cunning man a pound by instalments in 1875. James "Cunning" Murrell of Hadleigh, Essex (about 1785 to 1860), shoemaker and self-styled Devil's Master, kept a magic mirror, a telescope he said saw through walls and a copper charm that told honest clients from liars, and had iron witch bottles cast by the smith for nail parings, blood, urine and hair; the story goes that one exploded at midnight and the gypsy who had cursed a barking girl was found face down in her campfire. His death certificate says "Quack Doctor," 16 December 1860.

## The witch bottle
Their commonest weapon was the witch bottle, and we know exactly what went into one because in 2004 archaeologists in Greenwich dug up a salt-glazed Bartmann jug, buried neck-down a metre and a half deep, still corked. It splashed and rattled. X-rays showed pins and nails in the neck; CT showed it half full of liquid. Dr Alan Massey of Loughborough drew the liquid through the cork: human urine, with cotinine in it, so the maker smoked. Inside were twelve iron nails, the longest nine centimetres, eight brass pins, all deliberately bent; ten neatly pared fingernail clippings; a heart cut from leather and pierced with a nail; locks of dark brown hair carrying eighteen head lice; navel fluff; and a lump of sulphur that had reacted with the nails into iron sulphide. The theory, written down by cunning folk in the seventeenth century, was sympathetic and brutal: the witch's spell had tied her to her victim, so the victim's urine, nails and hair, stuck with bent pins and buried, would burn and tear in the witch's bladder until she lifted the curse. This is the only bottle ever found full.

The first printed recipe is Joseph Glanvill's Saducismus Triumphatus (1681): a languishing Suffolk wife, haunted by a shape like a bird, until "an old man" told her husband to cork her urine in a bottle with nails, pins and needles and bury it. By 2019, 294 witch bottles had been recorded in England. The Northampton Museum's Concealed Shoe Index, begun by June Swann, stands at 2,980 finds and more than 3,000 shoes, nearly always one worn, patched shoe in a chimney breast, under a floor by a door or in a roof. Dried cats, 139 documented by 2004, come from the same places, one bricked into a seventeenth-century cottage wall near Pendle Hill and found in 2011. Nobody wrote down why; the finds are the only text.

## Poppets and pictures of clay
The image spell was the one that got people hanged. In the North Berwick trials Agnes Sampson, the "wise wife of Keith," confessed under torture in 1590 that the Earl of Bothwell had asked her to make a wax image of King James; her dittay records a "picture of wax" enchanted "for his destruction" to be slipped under the bed sheet or bed head of the target. At Pendle in 1612 the confessions describe the same thing in clay. Old Demdike's examination, taken on 2 April and printed by Thomas Potts in 1613, says "that the speediest way to take a mans life away by Witchcraft, is to make a Picture of Clay, like vnto the shape of the person whom they meane to kill, & dry it thorowly: and when they would haue them to be ill in any one place more then an other; then take a Thorne or Pinne, and pricke it in that part of the Picture you would so haue to be ill," and that to kill outright they burned the picture. A witness had seen old Chattox in her garden with "a picture of Clay like vnto a child in her Apron." Pins drove the harm; fire finished it.

At Auldearn in 1662 Isobel Gowdie, in confessions a notary wrote down in her own voice, described a clay figure made to kill her landlord's child: the words over it said it would not break until struck with an axe and would last a hundred years, and it was set in the embers and roasted a little "every other day." At Bideford in 1682 Elizabeth Eastchurch found nine pricks in her sister Grace Thomas's knee and confronted Temperance Lloyd, who answered that she had pricked a piece of leather nine times; Lloyd, Mary Trembles and Susannah Edwards were hanged at Heavitree, Exeter, on 25 August 1682, among the last executed for witchcraft in England. At Salem on 2 June 1692 John Bly and his son testified that, taking down Bridget Bishop's cellar wall seven years before, they had found between the stones "several poppets made up of rags and hogs' bristles with headless pins in them with the points outward"; they never produced them. She was hanged on 10 June 1692, the first of the Salem dead.

## Love philtres
The oldest named love drug is hippomanes: Aristotle mentions it about 350 BC, and Pliny describes a growth on a newborn foal's forehead which the mare eats, and will not love the foal if prevented, so love itself was thought to live in it. Burchard, bishop of Worms, listed the sins confessors should ask about in his Corrector (about 1008 to 1012): a woman who lies face down, has bread kneaded on her bare buttocks, bakes it and feeds it to her husband so that he burns with love, two years' penance; a woman who kills a live fish in her birth passage, cooks it and feeds it to him, two years. In about AD 158 Apuleius stood trial for magic at Sabratha, accused of paying fishermen for rare fish with suggestive names to brew a philtre for the rich widow Pudentilla; his Apologia is the only speech from an ancient magic trial that survives.

## America: the Long Lost Friend, the hex murder and hoodoo
In 1820 Johann Georg Hohman of Reading, Pennsylvania, printed Der lange verborgene Freund, the Long Lost Friend, pow-wow remedies from older German books, each said three times. To stop bleeding: "I walk through a green forest; there I find three wells, cool and cold; the first is called courage, the second is called good, and the third is called stop the blood." For a burn, "Burn, I blow on thee," blown three times in one breath. Its last page promises: "Whoever carries this book with him, is safe from all his enemies, visible or invisible."

That promise killed a man. In York County in 1928 John Blymire, who believed himself hexed, was told by Nellie Noll, the River Witch of Marietta, to look at his palm where a dollar bill had lain; he saw the face of Nelson Rehmeyer, a pow-wower of Rehmeyer's Hollow, and was told to bury a lock of his hair eight feet deep or burn his Long Lost Friend. On the night of 27 November 1928 Blymire and two teenagers, John Curry and Wilbert Hess, beat and strangled Rehmeyer and fired the house; it would not burn, which the hollow took as proof of his power. At the January 1929 trials the motive was entered as robbery, the word witchcraft appearing nowhere; Blymire and Curry got life, Hess ten to twenty years.

Hoodoo, conjure or rootwork works through a mojo hand, a flannel bag of roots, lodestone, coins, pins, hair or a written petition, "fed" with whiskey to keep it alive; High John the Conqueror, a morning glory tuber, for court cases; and goofer dust, graveyard dirt cut in some recipes with salt, pepper, gunpowder, sulphur, ground bones and anvil dust, taken from a chosen grave only after coins or whiskey are left. Harry Middleton Hyatt recorded about 1,600 root workers between 1936 and 1940, often secretly, and published 13,458 spells and beliefs in five volumes between 1970 and 1978.

## The shape of a working
Put the sources side by side and every working, from Thebes in the fourth century to a Lancashire cottage in 1612 to a Pennsylvania hollow in 1928, has the same bones. A link to the target: a name and mother's name, hair, nails, urine, a figure in their likeness. A time: a planetary hour, the Moon in a mansion, a dark moon, nine days of fasting. A boundary: a circle nine feet across with God's names on its rim, a triangle for the thing to stand in, a threshold, a hearth, a grave. Words spoken or written, repeated three or nine times, in a language made strange by foreign names, Beralanensis, Erce, Adrieb, and often sung into the ear. A smell: frankincense, asafoetida, a cat's head on the coals, urine coming to the boil. An act on the body of the figure: bending, piercing, burning, drowning, roasting a little every other day. And a hiding place, so the thing goes on working unseen: lead in a spring, a bottle under the hearth, a shoe in the chimney, a lock of hair eight feet down. The workings were never vague. That is what makes them frightening: someone measured the circle.
