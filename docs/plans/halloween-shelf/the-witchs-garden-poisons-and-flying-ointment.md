# The Witch's Garden: Poisons and the Flying Ointment

## The nightshade family: henbane, belladonna, mandrake, datura
Four of the witch's plants are one chemistry. Henbane (Hyoscyamus niger), deadly nightshade (Atropa belladonna), mandrake (Mandragora officinarum) and the thornapples (Datura) all carry the tropane alkaloids hyoscyamine, atropine and scopolamine, which block the muscarinic receptors of the parasympathetic nerves. The poisoned body reads like the medical students' rhyme: hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter. Sweating stops, so the skin burns and flushes; the pupils blow wide and will not focus; the mouth dries until speech slurs; the heart races; the bladder locks. Then the mind goes: not coloured patterns but true delirium, conversations with people who are not there, picking at invisible threads, and no memory of it afterwards.

The best eyewitness account is Robert Beverley's History and Present State of Virginia (1705). British soldiers sent to put down Bacon's Rebellion at Jamestown in 1676 boiled the young leaves of a local Datura as a salad. For eleven days they were "natural fools": one blew a feather in the air, another sat naked in a corner "like a monkey, grinning and making mouths at them," a third kissed and pawed his companions.

Belladonna's name was recorded by Mattioli in 1544 from Italian women's use of the plant; Linnaeus added Atropa, the Fate who cuts the thread. The story that Venetian women dripped the juice into their eyes to widen their pupils is everywhere, but the Royal College of Physicians' garden notes that no sixteenth- or seventeenth-century source mentions dilated pupils.

The mandrake root forks like a body, which is where its legend starts. Josephus in The Jewish War describes a root called Baaras near Machaerus that killed whoever pulled it: dig round it until it hangs by a thread, tie a dog to it, walk away, and the dog tears it free and dies in your place. The scream that kills came later; by Shakespeare's day it was proverbial, "shrieks like mandrakes torn out of the earth" (Romeo and Juliet, IV.iii).

## Aconite, the queen of poisons
Monkshood or wolfsbane (Aconitum napellus) carries aconitine, which jams open the sodium channels of nerve and heart muscle. Ten to twenty minutes after eating it the mouth and face tingle and go numb, then burn; vomiting and cramping follow; the numbness spreads down the limbs; the heart stutters into arrhythmias that drugs barely touch. Death in a fatal case comes in two to six hours, usually from the heart, with the victim conscious. A few milligrams of the pure alkaloid kill an adult, and the root can pass for horseradish.

## Hemlock and Socrates
Poison hemlock (Conium maculatum) looks like wild carrot and smells of mouse. Its alkaloid coniine works on the nicotinic receptors: first stimulation, then a paralysis climbing from the feet. Plato's Phaedo describes it in 399 BC: the jailer told Socrates to walk until his legs felt heavy, then lie down; the man pressed his foot and asked if he felt it, and he said no; the coldness crept to the thighs and the belly, and when it reached the heart he would be gone. A 2024 toxicology paper concludes the Phaedo is a trimmed but genuine case report, with the drooling, retching and twitching of real hemlock poisoning left out to suit the death of a philosopher.

## Yew
Every part of the yew (Taxus baccata) is poisonous except the red flesh of the aril. Its taxine alkaloids attack the heart muscle directly, and a poisoned animal or person shows little before collapsing. Caesar records (Gallic War 6.31) that Catuvolcus, old king of the Eburones, "taxo, cuius magna in Gallia Germaniaque copia est, se exanimavit": killed himself with yew, of which Gaul and Germany have a great supply, rather than endure flight from the Romans in 53 BC.

## Foxglove
Foxglove (Digitalis purpurea) carries the cardiac glycosides digitoxin and digoxin, which strengthen the heartbeat at one dose and stop it at a slightly larger one. William Withering of Birmingham learned of it in 1775 from an old woman in Shropshire; he worked out that foxglove was the one that mattered, that the dried powdered leaf was five times stronger than fresh, and published An Account of the Foxglove in 1785 with 156 cases, including the ones that went wrong: nausea, a slow stumbling pulse, yellow-green haloes round lights, and a heart that will not restart.

## The flying ointment, and why flight is a drug effect
Giambattista della Porta of Naples published Magia Naturalis in 1558 and in Book 2, chapter 26, "Lamiarum Unguenta," printed what he said the witches used. His first list: the fat of children, water parsnip, aconite, poplar leaves and soot. His second: water parsnip, yellow flag iris, cinquefoil, bat's blood, nightshade and oil. Cardano had printed a similar list in De Subtilitate in 1550. Porta wrote that the women rubbed their skin hard until it was warm and red, then greased themselves so the plant juices went in, and believed they flew on moonlit nights to banquets where they ate, danced and had sex with young men of their choosing. He claimed to have watched one through a door: she smeared herself, fell into a sleep so deep that shouting, shaking and beating did not wake her, and on waking described her flight and the feast, though she had not moved. Johann Weyer used the recipe in De Praestigiis Daemonum (1563) to argue that witches were not flying anywhere; they were drugged, sick or deluded. The Latin edition went on the Spanish Index in 1583, and the 1589 edition quietly dropped the chapter.

The pharmacology fits. Rubbed into warmed skin, the tropane alkaloids of henbane and nightshade are absorbed, and scopolamine in particular gives a heavy sleep with vivid dream-delirium: weightlessness, rising and travelling, bodies and feasts, then waking exhausted and convinced. The English confessions say it in their own words. Elizabeth Style of Stoke Trister, Somerset, told the magistrate Robert Hunt in 1664 that before the meetings "they anoint their foreheads and hand-wrists with an oil the spirit brings them, which smells raw; and then they are carried in a very short time," saying as they went "Thout, tout a tout, tout, throughout and about." The sensation of flying is the plant working, and the authorities who read Porta and Weyer understood that, which is why they banned the book.

## Famous poisoners
Locusta of Gaul was a convicted poisoner under guard in Rome when Agrippina had her fetched in AD 54. Tacitus (Annals 12.66) says she dressed a dish of mushrooms, Claudius's favourite; when the emperor only sickened, his physician Xenophon pushed a feather down his throat to make him vomit, and the feather was poisoned too. In AD 55 Nero had her out again for his stepbrother Britannicus (Annals 13.15-17). The first draught only purged the boy; Nero flogged her and stood over her while she cooked a stronger one. At dinner the taster sipped the hot drink, Britannicus asked for it cooled, and the cold water added was the poison: he "immediately lost alike both voice and breath."

Aqua Tofana was a clear, tasteless water sold in Sicily and Rome in the seventeenth century in bottles labelled Manna di San Nicola, with a picture of the saint, so it could sit on a dressing table. The earliest record is 1632-33, when Francesca la Sarda and Teofania di Adamo were executed in Palermo for poisoning husbands with it. Giulia Tofana, probably her daughter, carried the trade to Rome. A few drops in soup over several days gave a lingering illness a doctor read as fever. Giulia seems to have died in 1651; the ring that outlived her, under Girolama Spana, was broken in 1659, when five women were hanged in Rome before an enormous crowd and more than forty customers were jailed for life. The six hundred dead husbands are a later tradition no record supports; Spana's own boast is recorded: "I've given this liquid to more people than I've got hairs on my head."
