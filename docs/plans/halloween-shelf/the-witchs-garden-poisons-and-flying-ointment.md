# The Witch's Garden: Poisons and the Flying Ointment

## The nightshade family: henbane, belladonna, mandrake, datura
Four of the witch's plants are one chemistry. Henbane (Hyoscyamus niger), deadly nightshade (Atropa belladonna), mandrake (Mandragora officinarum) and the thornapples (Datura) all carry the tropane alkaloids hyoscyamine, atropine and scopolamine, which block the muscarinic nerve receptors. The poisoned body reads like the medical students' rhyme: hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter. Sweating stops, so the skin burns and flushes; the pupils blow wide and will not focus; the mouth dries until speech slurs; the heart races; the bladder locks. Then the mind goes: not coloured patterns but true delirium, conversations with people who are not there, and no memory of it afterwards.

Henbane grows on waste ground, ruins and shingle by the sea, 30 to 100 cm, the whole plant clammy with sticky hairs and foul-smelling, the leaves grey-green and coarsely toothed. The flowers sit in a one-sided row, 2.5 to 4.5 cm across, five rounded lobes, dull pale yellow netted with purple veins and dark purple in the throat; each becomes a lidded capsule inside the swollen calyx, so a dead stem is a row of little urns. Its hyoscine hanged Dr Crippen in 1910.

Belladonna is a bushy perennial of chalk woodland edges, 1 to 1.5 m, with dull green oval leaves and single drooping bells 2.5 cm long, brownish-purple shading to yellow-green at the base, five-lobed. The berry is glossy black, 1 to 1.5 cm, sweet, one to a stalk, in a green five-pointed calyx wider than the fruit; two to five are given as a lethal adult dose, two or three for a child, though those are estimates. Mattioli recorded the name in 1544; Linnaeus added Atropa, the Fate who cuts the thread. The story that Venetian women dripped the juice into their eyes is everywhere, but the Royal College of Physicians' garden notes that no sixteenth- or seventeenth-century source mentions dilated pupils. Black nightshade (Solanum nigrum), the mostly harmless look-alike, has white stars with a yellow anther cone and dull black berries of 6 to 8 mm in bunches.

The best eyewitness account of datura is Robert Beverley's History and Present State of Virginia (1705). British soldiers sent to put down Bacon's Rebellion at Jamestown in 1676 boiled the young leaves of a local Datura as a salad. For eleven days they were "natural fools": one blew a feather in the air, another sat naked in a corner "like a monkey, grinning and making mouths at them," a third kissed and pawed his companions. Datura stramonium is a coarse annual 1 to 1.5 m with leaves that stink when touched and trumpets 6 to 10 cm long, white or pale violet, five-pointed, opening sweet-scented at evening for moths; the fruit is a spiny egg that splits four ways on 600 to 700 seeds, each with about a tenth of a milligram of atropine. Datura wrightii, the sacred datura of the American South-West, is a grey-green velvety mound with white trumpets up to 20 cm long that open at dusk for hawkmoths; the Chumash called it momoy.

## The mandrake: how to pull it, and what the texts say
Mandrake is a stemless Mediterranean perennial: a rosette of dark green wrinkled leaves up to 30 cm, greenish-white bells stained purple in spring, a yellow-orange berry the size of a ping-pong ball with a strange sweet smell, and a thick root sometimes forked like a body, which is where its legend starts. In 1990 Spanish doctors reported fifteen people in two families who had eaten its leaves in chard: symptoms began one to four hours after the meal, all had blurred vision, dry mouth, flushing, fixed wide pupils and a racing heart, nine were delirious, and recovery took 3 to 36 hours.

The pulling ritual is older than the scream. Theophrastus (Enquiry into Plants 9.8) says the diggers draw three circles round it with a sword, cut it facing west, and dance round it talking of the mysteries of love; Pliny (25.94) adds that the wind must not blow in the digger's face. Josephus (Jewish War 7.6.3) describes a root called Baaras near Machaerus, "its colour like that of flame," that killed whoever pulled it: dig round it until it hangs by a thread, tie a dog to it, walk away, and the dog tears it free and "dies immediately, as if it were instead of the man." Dog and mandrake first meet in the Vienna Dioscorides of about 512, where the nymph Heuresis hands Dioscorides a man-shaped root with a dying dog at her feet. The Old English Herbarium (eleventh century) gives the method in full: mark round the plant with iron, dig with an ivory wand so iron never touches the root, bind its hands and feet, tie the cord to a hungry dog's neck and throw meat beyond its reach. The death-scream reaches Europe only from the twelfth century, first written, folklore writers say, in Philippe de Thaon's bestiary of about 1120; by Shakespeare's day it was proverbial, "shrieks like mandrakes torn out of the earth" (Romeo and Juliet, IV.iii). The gallows legend, that the root springs from a hanged man's drops, enters print about 1500, when the surgeon Brunschwig already called it false; in 1630 three women were executed in Hamburg for owning Alraun root figures.

## Aconite, the queen of poisons
Monkshood or wolfsbane (Aconitum napellus) grows 1 to 1.5 m in damp mountain meadows, with dark glossy leaves cut into five to seven narrow lobes and a spike of deep blue-violet flowers from June to August; the hood is the topmost of five sepals, the true petals hidden inside it. The root is a blackish tuber like a small turnip, and it can pass for horseradish. Its aconitine jams open the sodium channels of nerve and heart muscle; 2 to 6 mg kills an adult. Ten to twenty minutes after eating it the mouth and face tingle and go numb, then burn; vomiting and cramping follow; the numbness spreads down the limbs; the heart stutters into arrhythmias that drugs barely touch. Death in a fatal case comes in two to six hours, usually from the heart, with the victim conscious. Dr George Lamson gave it to his brother-in-law Percy John, 18, in a Dundee cake on 3 December 1881; the boy was retching dark fluid within the hour and dead that night, and the analyst Thomas Stevenson found the supposedly undetectable poison by tasting the extracts and feeling the tingling.

## Hemlock and Socrates
Poison hemlock (Conium maculatum) stands 1.5 to 2.5 m on waste ground and river banks, with smooth hollow stems blotched purple, fern-like leaves, flat white umbels in June and July, and a mousy smell when bruised; cow parsley and wild carrot have hairy stems and no blotches. Its coniine works on the nicotinic receptors: first stimulation, then a paralysis climbing from the feet with the mind clear until the breath stops. Plato's Phaedo describes it in 399 BC: the jailer told Socrates to walk until his legs felt heavy, then lie down; the man "pinched his foot hard and asked if he felt it. He said No; then after that, his thighs; and passing upwards in this way he showed us that he was growing cold and rigid," and when it reached his heart he would be gone. Enid Bloch argued in 2001 that the account fits coniine's ascending paralysis; a 2024 paper in Clinical Toxicology concludes the Phaedo is a trimmed but genuine case report, with the drooling, retching and twitching of real hemlock poisoning left out to suit the death of a philosopher.

## Yew
Every part of the yew (Taxus baccata) is poisonous except the red flesh of the aril, the fleshy cup, open at the tip, round one black seed. It is an evergreen up to 20 m with reddish-brown flaking bark and flat dark needles 1 to 3 cm long in two ranks. Its taxine alkaloids attack the heart directly; about 50 g of needles kills, and a poisoned animal or person shows little before collapsing. Caesar records (Gallic War 6.31) that Catuvolcus, old king of the Eburones, "taxo, cuius magna in Gallia Germaniaque copia est, se exanimavit": killed himself with yew, of which Gaul and Germany have a great supply, rather than endure flight from the Romans in 53 BC.

## Foxglove
Foxglove (Digitalis purpurea) carries the cardiac glycosides digitoxin and digoxin, which strengthen the heartbeat at one dose and stop it at a slightly larger one. It is a biennial of acid soils and woodland clearings: a rosette of soft downy leaves in the first year, then a spike 1 to 2 m with 20 to 100 hanging tubes 4 to 5.5 cm long all facing one way, purple or sometimes white, the throat spotted dark purple with each spot ringed in white to guide bumblebees. William Withering of Birmingham learned of it in 1775 from an old woman in Shropshire; he worked out that foxglove was the one that mattered, that the dried powdered leaf was five times stronger than fresh, and published An Account of the Foxglove in 1785 with 156 cases, including the ones that went wrong: nausea, a slow stumbling pulse, yellow-green haloes round lights, and a heart that will not restart.

## Hellebore and ergot
White hellebore (Veratrum album) is a lily relative of alpine meadows, 50 to 175 cm, broad pleated leaves clasping the stem, a branched spike of greenish-white six-pointed flowers; people take its root for yellow gentian, and its veratridine brings vomiting and a crawling pulse. The true hellebores are buttercups: the Christmas rose Helleborus niger, white five-sepalled flowers flushing pink from January, and the stinking hellebore Helleborus foetidus, drooping pale green cups rimmed purple, rank when bruised, both with the cardiac glycoside hellebrin. Pausanias (10.36) says the Greeks sent their mad to Anticyra, where "black hellebore sends those who take it to stool, the white kind purges by vomiting." Claviceps purpurea replaces grains of rye with hard purple-black sclerotia 1 to 3 cm long, curved like a cock's spur, carrying ergotamine and lysergic acid amides. Gangrenous ergotism clamps the blood vessels shut so the limbs burn, go cold, then black, and the feet and fingers drop off; the convulsive form brings fits, mania and visions. The Aquitaine epidemic of 994 is put at 20,000 to 40,000 dead.

## The flying ointment, and why flight is a drug effect
The earliest physical trace is Kilkenny, 1324: Holinshed's chronicle (1577) says the bishop's men found in Dame Alice Kyteler's closet "a Pipe of oyntment, wherewith she greased a staffe, upon the which she ambled and galloped through thick and thin." The earliest recipe is Johannes Hartlieb's Book of All Forbidden Arts (1456): seven herbs each picked on its own weekday, mixed with birds' blood and animal fat, the seventh withheld so no one is harmed. Andres Laguna, town physician of Metz, wrote in his 1555 Dioscorides that in 1545 the house of a couple condemned for witchcraft yielded "a pot half full of a green ointment" smelling of hemlock, nightshade, henbane and mandrake; he had the hangman's sleepless wife rubbed with it, she slept thirty-six hours and had to be beaten awake, and her first words were an angry "Why in an evil hour did you wake me?" Cardano's De Subtilitate (1550) lists children's fat, so they say, parsley, aconite, cinquefoil, nightshade and soot, and says the women see banquets, young men, kings, and also demons, ravens and prisons.

Giambattista della Porta of Naples published Magia Naturalis in 1558 and in Book 2, chapter 26, "Lamiarum Unguenta," printed what he said the witches used. In Reginald Scot's English (1584): "The fat of young children, and seeth it with water in a brazen vessel... They put hereunto Eleoselinum, Aconitum, Frondes populeas, and Soote. Another receipt to the same purpose. Sium, acarum vulgare, pentaphyllon, the blood of a flitter-mouse, solanum somniferum, and oleum," that is water parsnip, sweet flag, cinquefoil, bat's blood and nightshade. "They rub all parts of their bodies exceedingly, till they looke red, and be very hot... in a moone-light night they seeme to be carried in the aire, to feasting, singing, dancing, kissing, culling, and other acts of venery, with such youthes as they love and desire most." He claimed to have watched one through a chink in a door: she anointed herself and "fell downe... into a most sound and heavy sleep," and on waking "affirmed that she had passed over both seas and mountaines." Johann Weyer used the recipe in De Praestigiis Daemonum (1563) to argue that witches were not flying anywhere: the ointments "produce a sleep most like death, filled with vivid dreams." The Latin Magia went on the Spanish Index in 1583, and the 1589 edition quietly dropped the chapter. Pierre de Lancre's Tableau (1612), from the Basque burnings of 1609, makes the fat of unbaptised babies its base.

The pharmacology fits. Rubbed into warmed skin, the tropane alkaloids are absorbed: the Transderm Scop patch delivers a milligram of scopolamine through the skin over three days. Scopolamine gives a heavy sleep with vivid dream-delirium: weightlessness, rising and travelling, bodies and feasts, then waking exhausted and convinced. Will-Erich Peuckert published in 1960 how he and a friend had tried a Porta-derived salve: both slept about twenty hours and wrote separate accounts that matched, wild rides through the air, falls, and an orgiastic feast at the end. Animal transformation belongs to the same delirium: psychiatrists have recorded patients convinced they were turning into wolves during drug intoxication.

The English confessions say it in their own words. Elizabeth Style of Stoke Trister, Somerset, told the magistrate Robert Hunt in January 1664 that she, Alice Duke and Anne Bishop met at night "in the Common near Trister Gate, where they met a Man in black Clothes with a little Band," stuck thorns into a wax image and "had Wine, Cakes and Roastmeat." Before the meetings "they anoint their Foreheads, and Hand-wrists with an Oyl the Spirit brings them (which smells raw) and then they are carried in a very short time, using these words as they pass, Thout, tout a tout, tout, throughout and about. And when they go off from their Meetings, they say, Rentum Tormentum." The sensation of flying is the plant working, and the authorities who read Porta and Weyer understood that, which is why they banned the book.

## Famous poisoners
Locusta of Gaul was a convicted poisoner under guard in Rome when Agrippina had her fetched in AD 54. Tacitus (Annals 12.66) says she dressed a dish of mushrooms, Claudius's favourite; when the emperor only sickened, his physician Xenophon pushed a feather down his throat to make him vomit, and the feather was poisoned too. In AD 55 Nero had her out again for his stepbrother Britannicus (Annals 13.15-17). The first draught only purged the boy; Suetonius (Nero 33) says Nero flogged her with his own hand and stood over her while she cooked a stronger one, tried it on a kid, which lingered five hours, boiled it down again and threw it to a pig, which fell dead on the spot. At dinner the taster sipped the hot drink, Britannicus asked for it cooled, and the cold water added was the poison: he "immediately lost alike both voice and breath."

Aqua Tofana was a clear, tasteless water sold in Sicily and Rome in the seventeenth century in bottles labelled Manna di San Nicola, with a picture of the saint, so it could sit on a dressing table. The earliest record is 1632-33, when Francesca la Sarda and Teofania di Adamo were executed in Palermo for poisoning husbands with it. Nineteenth-century writers made Giulia Tofana her daughter; Craig Monson's 2020 archival study finds no record of the link. A few drops in soup over several days gave a lingering illness a doctor read as fever. The Roman ring was broken in 1659: on 5 July Girolama Spana and four other women were hanged in Rome, and more than forty customers were exiled or confined for life. The six hundred dead husbands are an eighteenth-century claim, and Spana's boast, "I've given this liquid to more people than I've got hairs on my head," a later telling.

Paris had the Affair of the Poisons. Catherine Monvoisin, La Voisin, a fortune-teller and abortionist, sold "poudres de succession," inheritance powders, which were arsenic, to several hundred clients. Louis XIV set up the Chambre Ardente in April 1679; she was arrested on 12 March 1679 after Mass, had her legs crushed in the brodequins without confessing, and was burned alive on the Place de Greve on 22 February 1680. Madame de Sevigne wrote the next day that at midnight she "drank a lot of wine, she sang twenty drinking songs," and that at the stake "she swore a great deal, she pushed away the straw five or six times; but at last the fire grew, and she was lost to sight." Her daughter then told of black masses said over a naked woman's belly for Madame de Montespan, the king's mistress; nothing but confessions supported it, the court executed 34, and in 1709 the king burned the papers himself.

Mary Ann Cotton, born in County Durham on 31 October 1832, is believed to have poisoned eleven of her thirteen children, three of her four husbands, her mother and her lover with arsenic, which the doctors certified as gastric fever: vomiting, a burning throat, cramps and the watery "rice-water" stools of cholera, over days. Her last victim was her seven-year-old stepson Charles Edward Cotton, who died at West Auckland on 12 July 1872, days after she told the relief officer Thomas Riley that he would "go like all the rest of the Cotton family." Dr Kilburn kept the boy's stomach, and the Reinsch test plated its arsenic grey on copper. At Durham Assizes in March 1873 her counsel blamed the green floral wallpaper, and Charles Russell showed that ten of the dead had never slept in that room. William Calcraft hanged her on 24 March 1873 with too short a drop, pressing on her shoulders while she took about three minutes to strangle. The skipping rhyme goes "Mary Ann Cotton, she's dead and she's rotten, lying in bed with her eyes wide open."
