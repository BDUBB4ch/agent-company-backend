# Witch Trials, Torture and Execution

## The numbers
From about 1400 to 1775 roughly 100,000 people were prosecuted for witchcraft in Europe and British America and between 40,000 and 60,000 were executed, three-quarters or more of them women. The worst years were 1580 to 1630. The "nine million" that circulates online came from nineteenth-century polemic and has no basis in court records.

Brian Levack's survey of the records (2006) puts prosecutions at about 90,000 and executions at about 45,000. The German lands account for about 25,000 executions, the largest share anywhere. The Survey of Scottish Witchcraft at Edinburgh (Julian Goodare and Lauren Martin) lists 3,837 accused between 1563 and 1736, 84 per cent of the named ones women, and reckons about 2,500 executed. England, which allowed no judicial torture and hanged rather than burned, executed fewer than 500 in three centuries.

## The handbook
In 1485 the Dominican inquisitor Heinrich Kramer put fourteen people on trial at Innsbruck, chief among them Helena Scheuberin, a burgher's wife who had insulted him in the street and walked out of his sermons; the bishop wrote that Kramer "presumed much that had not been proved" and ordered him out of the diocese. Kramer went home and wrote the Malleus Maleficarum, the Hammer of Witches, finished in 1486 and printed at Speyer with Innocent VIII's bull of 1484 in the preface. It ran through twenty-eight editions by 1600. Its argument: witchcraft is real, it is mostly women, torture is lawful to obtain confession, and death is "the only sure remedy."

## Trier, 1581 to 1593
The canon Linden, an eyewitness, wrote that "scarcely any of those who were accused escaped punishment," and that the judge, two burgomasters, councillors, canons and parish priests "were swept away in this ruin." His count is 368 burned in twenty-two villages between 1587 and 1593. The chief judge Dietrich Flade, who had sentenced leniently, was named by tortured prisoners, tortured through five sessions himself, and strangled and burned on 18 September 1589.

Linden records who prospered: the executioner "rode a blooded horse, like a noble of the court, and went clad in gold and silver; his wife vied with noble dames in the richness of her array."

## Fulda, 1603 to 1606
About 250 died at Fulda in three years under the judge Balthasar Nuss. Merga Bien, twice widowed and twice an heiress, was arrested on 19 June 1603. Her husband told the Imperial Chamber Court she was pregnant and could not lawfully be tortured; the court turned it round, fourteen years married without a child and now expecting, so the child was the Devil's. She confessed under torture to poisoning her second husband and their children and was burned at Fulda that autumn; her husband was sent the bill.

## Würzburg and Bamberg, 1626 to 1631
In May 1626 a late frost killed the Franconian harvest. Prince-Bishop Philipp Adolf von Ehrenberg of Würzburg then burned around 900 people in five years, among them his own nephew, between nineteen and forty-three priests, and children as young as seven made to say they had lain with demons. At Bamberg, Prince-Bishop Fuchs von Dornheim built the Drudenhaus in 1627, a purpose-built witch prison with its own torture rooms; about 300 from the city and 600 from the countryside were executed before Swedish troops approached in 1632, and the survivors were freed on an oath of silence about what had been done to them.

A printed list of the Würzburg burnings names what it can. The fifth burning: "the fat tailor's wife." The eighth: "Baunach, a senator, the fattest citizen in Würzburg." The thirteenth: an old woman, "a little girl nine or ten years old" and "a younger girl, her little sister." It stops at the twenty-ninth burning and calls itself "far from complete." In August 1629 the prince-bishop's own chancellor wrote to a friend that "there are still four hundred in the city, high and low, of every rank and sex, nay, even clerics, so strongly accused that they may be arrested at any hour," and: "I have seen put to death children of seven, promising students of ten, twelve, fourteen, and fifteen."

Dorothea Flock, a councillor's wife, was put back into the Drudenhaus six weeks after childbirth, confessed under torture, and was sentenced on 14 May 1630 to be torn with red-hot pincers and burned alive; the Emperor's order to free her, dated 11 May, arrived five days after she died on 17 May.

Johannes Junius, mayor of Bamberg, was examined on 30 June 1628. The minute reads: thumbscrews, "feels no pain"; leg-screws; hoisted in the strappado, arms bound behind, still denying. On 5 July he confessed. On 24 July he paid a jailer a thaler to carry a letter to his daughter: "Many hundred thousand good-nights, dearly beloved daughter Veronica. Innocent have I come into prison, innocent have I been tortured, innocent must I die. For whoever comes into the witch prison must become a witch or be tortured until he invents something out of his head." The executioner, he wrote, had begged him: "Sir, I pray you, for God's sake confess something, whether it be true or not. Invent something, for you cannot endure the torture which you will be put to." He was burned on 6 August 1628.

The Jesuit Friedrich Spee, confessor to the condemned, published the Cautio Criminalis anonymously in 1631: "Torture has the power to create witches where none exist."

## North Berwick, 1590 to 1591
It began in November 1590 with Geillis Duncan, a servant at Tranent whose master crushed her fingers in the pilliwinks, a thumbscrew, and had her head bound and wrenched with a cord; stripped and searched, she was found marked on the throat, and named about seventy people. Agnes Sampson, the "wise wife of Keith," was questioned at Holyrood before James VI himself. The London pamphlet Newes from Scotland (1591) says she "had all her hair shaven off, in each part of her body, and her head thrawen with a rope according to the custom of that country, being a pain most grievous, which she continued almost an hour," that she was kept from sleep in a witch's bridle, an iron frame with four prongs forced into the mouth, and confessed when the Devil's mark was found on her private parts: two hundred witches in the kirk at North Berwick, the Devil in the pulpit. She was strangled and burned on Castle Hill, Edinburgh, on 28 January 1591. The schoolmaster John Fian had the pilliwinks, then the boots, a vice round the leg tightened with hammer blows, and was burned in January 1591.

James wrote Daemonologie out of it, printed at Edinburgh in 1597 as a dialogue. Philomathes asks why there are "twentie women given to that craft, where ther is one man," and Epistemon answers: "for as that sexe is frailer then man is, so is it easier to be intrapped in these grosse snares of the Devill." In the hunt of 1597 Aberdeen's burgh accounts for burning Janet Wishart and Isobel Cocker itemise peats, coals, four tar barrels, four fathoms of rope, a stake, and 13 shillings 4 pence to Jon Justice for the execution.

## Pendle, 1612
A pedlar named John Law refused to sell pins to Alizon Device near Colne in March 1612; he collapsed, lamed down one side, and she believed she had cursed him. Twelve people, most from two feuding families of healers, the Demdikes and the Chattoxes, and among them the gentlewoman Alice Nutter, were charged. Old Demdike, in her eighties and blind, died in the Lancaster Castle dungeon before trial. The star witness was nine-year-old Jennet Device, who stood on a table and named her mother Elizabeth, her brother James, her sister Alizon and six others; English courts did not take evidence from children under fourteen, but James I's Daemonologie held that in witchcraft the usual protections did not apply. Jennet Preston had already been hanged at York on 29 July. On 20 August 1612 the other nine, Alizon, Elizabeth and James Device, Anne Whittle (Chattox) and her daughter Anne Redferne, Alice Nutter, Katherine Hewitt, and John and Jane Bulcock, were hanged together on the moor above Lancaster. Fewer than 500 people were executed for witchcraft in England in three centuries; this one morning was two per cent of them.

The record is The Wonderfull Discoverie of Witches in the Countie of Lancaster by Thomas Potts, clerk of the court (1613), which preserves Chattox's charm over bewitched drink, "Three Biters hast thou bitten, The Hart, ill Eye, ill Tonge," and the Good Friday dinner at Malkin Tower, the Demdike house, where the families met over a stolen sheep and, on Jennet's evidence alone, planned to blow up Lancaster Castle. In 1634 a ten-year-old, Edmund Robinson, started a second Pendle hunt on a story he later admitted inventing; among the nineteen tried was a Jennet Device of the right age and place, who folklore holds was the child witness of 1612, still in Lancaster Castle in 1636, and nothing more is known of her.

## Basque country, 1609 to 1614
The panic ran from the cave village of Zugarramurdi to the Inquisition at Logroño. At the auto-da-fé of 7 and 8 November 1610, before a crowd put at thirty thousand, six who would not confess were burned alive, and five were burned in effigy because they had died in the cells. In 1611 the inquisitor Alonso de Salazar Frías went out with an edict of grace, took 1,802 confessions, 1,384 of them from children aged seven to fourteen, and watched most of them retracted. His report: "there were neither witches nor bewitched in the district until they were talked and written about." The Supreme Council adopted his view, and Spain's Inquisition burned almost no one for witchcraft after.

## Matthew Hopkins, 1645 to 1647
On 21 March 1645 a Manningtree tailor swore that his neighbour Elizabeth Clarke, a one-legged woman of about forty, had bewitched his wife. Matthew Hopkins, a Puritan vicar's son no older than twenty-five, and John Stearne "watched" her: three days and nights without sleep, stripped and searched by women for teats, kept walking. On the third night she named her familiars, Holt, Jarmara, Vinegar Tom, Sack and Sugar and Newes, which Hopkins printed in The Discovery of Witches (1647). At Chelmsford nineteen were hanged and four died in jail. Across Essex, Suffolk, Norfolk and Huntingdonshire Hopkins and Stearne sent somewhere between 100 and 300 to the gallows at "twenty shillings a town" by Hopkins's own account, during a civil war with no assize judges riding the circuit. Hopkins died at Manningtree on 12 August 1647, probably of tuberculosis.

John Lowes, vicar of Brandeston, about seventy, was swum in a moat, kept awake three nights and walked until his feet blistered, and confessed. Eighteen were hanged at Bury St Edmunds on 27 August 1645, Lowes among them, reading his own burial service on the way.

## Swimming, pricking, watching
The swimming test bound thumbs to opposite big toes, ran a rope round the waist, and threw the accused into a pond. Water, James wrote in Daemonologie (1597), "shall refuse to receive them in her bosome, that have shaken off them the sacred Water of Baptisme." Float and you were guilty; sink and you were innocent, if the men on the rope were quick. Pricking searched for the Devil's mark, a mole, wart or patch of dead skin, stabbed with a needle after the body was shaved, to show it neither bled nor hurt; Scottish prickers were paid by the head, and some used retractable blades.

The last swimming in England was a lynching: Ruth Osborne, about seventy, and her husband John were taken out of Tring workhouse on 22 April 1751 by a crowd of four or five thousand, stripped, hands tied to toes, and dragged through a pond on a rope while a chimney sweep, Thomas Colley, turned her with a stick until she was dead on the bank. He was hanged for it in August 1751.

## The devices in the records
Continental torture ran up a ladder, each rung minuted by a clerk as in the Junius record: thumbscrews; the leg-screws or the boots; then the strappado, wrists bound behind, the rope over a beam, the body hoisted so the shoulders came out of joint, weights of fifty pounds and more hung from the feet. A spiked iron chair, the Hexenstuhl, survives from a seventeenth-century original in the Rothenburg crime museum, with a fire that could be lit beneath.

## Salem, 1692
Twenty people were executed and at least five more died in jail, among them Sarah Good's infant daughter Mercy. The nineteen hangings took place at Proctor's Ledge, a rocky shelf at the foot of Gallows Hill, confirmed as the site in 2016. Bridget Bishop, 10 June; Sarah Good, Rebecca Nurse, Elizabeth Howe, Susannah Martin and Sarah Wildes, 19 July; George Burroughs, George Jacobs Sr., Martha Carrier, John Proctor and John Willard, 19 August; Martha Corey, Mary Eastey, Mary Parker, Alice Parker, Ann Pudeator, Wilmot Redd, Margaret Scott and Samuel Wardwell, 22 September. Burroughs, the only minister hanged, recited the Lord's Prayer perfectly from the ladder, which a witch was held unable to do; by Robert Calef's account of 1700 the crowd wavered until Cotton Mather, on horseback, called out that the Devil had often been transformed into an angel of light. The story goes that Sarah Good told the Reverend Noyes from the ladder that if he took her life "God will give you blood to drink"; the line is in no contemporary record. What Noyes is recorded saying, on 22 September, looking at the hanging bodies: "What a sad thing it is to see eight firebrands of hell hanging there."

Giles Corey, eighty-one, refused to plead. On 17 September Sheriff Corwin had him stripped and laid under a board, and stones were piled on it. For two days the court tried to make him speak. Samuel Sewall's diary for 19 September: "Abt noon, at Salem, Giles Corey was pressed to death for standing mute." Legend holds his only words were "More weight." Because he was never convicted, his farm passed to his heirs. His wife Martha was hanged three days later.

Warrants went out on 29 February 1692 for the minister Samuel Parris's enslaved woman Tituba, the beggar Sarah Good and the bedridden Sarah Osborne. Tituba, whom Parris had beaten, confessed on 1 March to a tall man of Boston, a book she signed, a yellow bird and two cats, and set the pattern. Rebecca Nurse, seventy-one, said at her examination on 24 March: "I can say before my Eternal father I am innocent, and God will clear my innocency." Martha Carrier of Andover told the court: "It is a shameful thing that you should mind these folks that are out of their wits." Her sons and John Proctor's son William were tied neck and heels, Proctor wrote from prison on 23 July, "till the Blood gushed out at his Nose." Mary Eastey, Rebecca Nurse's sister, petitioned in her last week: "I Petition to your Honours not for my own Life, for I know I must die, and my appointed time is set; but the Lord he knows it is, if it be possible, that no more Innocent Blood be shed."

Calef gives the day: the condemned "carried in a cart with others, through the streets of Salem, to execution," and after Burroughs was cut down "he was dragged by a halter to a hole, or grave, between the rocks, about two feet deep; his shirt and breeches being pulled off, and an old pair of trousers of one executed put on his lower parts: he was so put in, together with Willard and Carrier, that one of his hands, and his chin, and a foot of one of them, was left uncovered."

## How executions went
In England and its colonies the sentence was hanging from a cart or ladder; the drop was short and death came by strangulation over minutes. In Scotland the record of 141 sentences specifying a method shows 120 for strangling and burning: a Dumfries sentence of 18 May 1671 orders two women "to be wirried at ane stake till they be dead And theirafter their bodies to be brunt to ashes." Janet Horne, the last in Scotland, was burned alive at Dornoch in 1727. On the continent Würzburg's victims were burned, "sometimes after having been beheaded or strangled, but other times alive."

From the prison the cart or the walk ran through the town to a moor or hill outside it, so the town lined the way. At the stake stood what the burgh had bought, at Aberdeen the peats, coals, tar barrels and rope to bind and to strangle. Sweden beheaded first and burned the body; at Torsåker on one day in 1675 seventy-one people, sixty-five women and six men, a fifth of the women of the district, were beheaded and burned. The smell was tar, peat smoke and burning hair; the sound was the crowd, the psalm, and whatever the condemned said.

## The last ones
Janet Horne of Dornoch, an old woman going senile, was charged in 1727 with riding her daughter, who had deformed hands and feet, to the Devil to be shod like a pony; the sheriff-depute ordered both burned next day, and the daughter escaped. The story goes that Janet was stripped, smeared with tar, carried through the town in a barrel and, not understanding, warmed her hands at the fire built for her; no court record survives.

The last person executed for witchcraft in Europe was Anna Göldi, at Glarus in Switzerland on 13 June 1782, a maidservant of forty-seven. Her master Johann Jakob Tschudi, a judge, said his daughter had vomited more than a hundred needles after Göldi put pins in her milk; the likelier background is an affair with Tschudi she was about to expose. In prison she was hung up by her thumbs with her hands bound behind her and weights tied to her ankles until she confessed a pact with the Devil, and withdrew it the moment the torture stopped. The sentence named poisoning, which carried no death penalty for a non-fatal case; she was beheaded with the sword. The case gave German the word Justizmord, judicial murder; Glarus declared her innocent in 2008.
