# Witch Trials, Torture and Execution

## The numbers
From about 1400 to 1775 roughly 100,000 people were prosecuted for witchcraft in Europe and British America and between 40,000 and 60,000 were executed, three-quarters or more of them women. The worst years were 1580 to 1630. The "nine million" that circulates online came from nineteenth-century polemic and has no basis in court records.

## The handbook
In 1485 the Dominican inquisitor Heinrich Kramer put fourteen people on trial at Innsbruck, chief among them Helena Scheuberin, a burgher's wife who had insulted him in the street and walked out of his sermons; the bishop wrote that Kramer "presumed much that had not been proved" and ordered him out of the diocese. Kramer went home and wrote the Malleus Maleficarum, the Hammer of Witches, finished in 1486 and printed at Speyer with Innocent VIII's bull of 1484 in the preface. It ran through twenty-eight editions by 1600. Its argument: witchcraft is real, it is mostly women, torture is lawful to obtain confession, and death is "the only sure remedy."

## Trier, 1581 to 1593
The canon Linden, an eyewitness, wrote that "scarcely any of those who were accused escaped punishment," and that the judge, two burgomasters, councillors, canons and parish priests "were swept away in this ruin." His count is 368 burned in twenty-two villages between 1587 and 1593. The chief judge Dietrich Flade, who had sentenced leniently, was named by tortured prisoners, tortured through five sessions himself, and strangled and burned on 18 September 1589.

## Würzburg and Bamberg, 1626 to 1631
In May 1626 a late frost killed the Franconian harvest. Prince-Bishop Philipp Adolf von Ehrenberg of Würzburg then burned around 900 people in five years, among them his own nephew, between nineteen and forty-three priests, and children as young as seven made to say they had lain with demons. At Bamberg, Prince-Bishop Fuchs von Dornheim built the Drudenhaus in 1627, a purpose-built witch prison with its own torture rooms; about 300 from the city and 600 from the countryside were executed before Swedish troops approached in 1632, and the survivors were freed on an oath of silence about what had been done to them.

Johannes Junius, mayor of Bamberg, was examined on 30 June 1628. The minute reads: thumbscrews, "feels no pain"; leg-screws; hoisted in the strappado, arms bound behind, still denying. On 5 July he confessed. On 24 July he paid a jailer a thaler to carry a letter to his daughter: "Many hundred thousand good-nights, dearly beloved daughter Veronica. Innocent have I come into prison, innocent have I been tortured, innocent must I die. For whoever comes into the witch prison must become a witch or be tortured until he invents something out of his head." The executioner, he wrote, had begged him: "Sir, I pray you, for God's sake confess something, whether it be true or not. Invent something, for you cannot endure the torture which you will be put to." He was burned on 6 August 1628.

## Pendle, 1612
A pedlar named John Law refused to sell pins to Alizon Device near Colne in March 1612; he collapsed, lamed down one side, and she believed she had cursed him. Twelve people, most from two feuding families of healers, the Demdikes and the Chattoxes, and among them the gentlewoman Alice Nutter, were charged. Old Demdike, in her eighties and blind, died in the Lancaster Castle dungeon before trial. The star witness was nine-year-old Jennet Device, who stood on a table and named her mother Elizabeth, her brother James, her sister Alizon and six others; English courts did not take evidence from children under fourteen, but James I's Daemonologie held that in witchcraft the usual protections did not apply. Jennet Preston had already been hanged at York on 29 July. On 20 August 1612 the other nine, Alizon, Elizabeth and James Device, Anne Whittle (Chattox) and her daughter Anne Redferne, Alice Nutter, Katherine Hewitt, and John and Jane Bulcock, were hanged together on the moor above Lancaster. Fewer than 500 people were executed for witchcraft in England in three centuries; this one morning was two per cent of them.

## Matthew Hopkins, 1645 to 1647
On 21 March 1645 a Manningtree tailor swore that his neighbour Elizabeth Clarke, a one-legged woman of about forty, had bewitched his wife. Matthew Hopkins, a Puritan vicar's son no older than twenty-five, and John Stearne "watched" her: three days and nights without sleep, stripped and searched by women for teats, kept walking. On the third night she named her familiars, Holt, Jarmara, Vinegar Tom, Sack and Sugar and Newes, which Hopkins printed in The Discovery of Witches (1647). At Chelmsford nineteen were hanged and four died in jail. Across Essex, Suffolk, Norfolk and Huntingdonshire Hopkins and Stearne sent somewhere between 100 and 300 to the gallows at "twenty shillings a town" by Hopkins's own account, during a civil war with no assize judges riding the circuit. Hopkins died at Manningtree on 12 August 1647, probably of tuberculosis.

## Swimming, pricking, watching
The swimming test bound thumbs to opposite big toes, ran a rope round the waist, and threw the accused into a pond. Water, James wrote in Daemonologie (1597), "shall refuse to receive them in her bosome, that have shaken off them the sacred Water of Baptisme." Float and you were guilty; sink and you were innocent, if the men on the rope were quick. Pricking searched for the Devil's mark, a mole, wart or patch of dead skin, stabbed with a needle after the body was shaved, to show it neither bled nor hurt; Scottish prickers were paid by the head, and some used retractable blades.

## How executions went
In England and its colonies the sentence was hanging from a cart or ladder; the drop was short and death came by strangulation over minutes. In Scotland the record of 141 sentences specifying a method shows 120 for strangling and burning: a Dumfries sentence of 18 May 1671 orders two women "to be wirried at ane stake till they be dead And theirafter their bodies to be brunt to ashes." Janet Horne, the last in Scotland, was burned alive at Dornoch in 1727. On the continent Würzburg's victims were burned, "sometimes after having been beheaded or strangled, but other times alive."

## Salem, 1692
Twenty people were executed and at least five more died in jail, among them Sarah Good's infant daughter Mercy. The nineteen hangings took place at Proctor's Ledge, a rocky shelf at the foot of Gallows Hill, confirmed as the site in 2016. Bridget Bishop, 10 June; Sarah Good, Rebecca Nurse, Elizabeth Howe, Susannah Martin and Sarah Wildes, 19 July; George Burroughs, George Jacobs Sr., Martha Carrier, John Proctor and John Willard, 19 August; Martha Corey, Mary Eastey, Mary Parker, Alice Parker, Ann Pudeator, Wilmot Redd, Margaret Scott and Samuel Wardwell, 22 September. Burroughs, the only minister hanged, recited the Lord's Prayer perfectly from the ladder, which a witch was held unable to do; by Robert Calef's account of 1700 the crowd wavered until Cotton Mather, on horseback, called out that the Devil had often been transformed into an angel of light. The story goes that Sarah Good told the Reverend Noyes from the ladder that if he took her life "God will give you blood to drink"; the line is in no contemporary record. What Noyes is recorded saying, on 22 September, looking at the hanging bodies: "What a sad thing it is to see eight firebrands of hell hanging there."

Giles Corey, eighty-one, refused to plead. On 17 September Sheriff Corwin had him stripped and laid under a board, and stones were piled on it. For two days the court tried to make him speak. Samuel Sewall's diary for 19 September: "Abt noon, at Salem, Giles Corey was pressed to death for standing mute." Legend holds his only words were "More weight." Because he was never convicted, his farm passed to his heirs. His wife Martha was hanged three days later.
