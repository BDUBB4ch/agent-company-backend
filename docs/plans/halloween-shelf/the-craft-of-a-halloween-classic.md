# The Craft of a Halloween Classic: What Gets Watched Every Year

## The six that people actually return to
It's the Great Pumpkin, Charlie Brown (CBS, 27 October 1966) was the first Halloween special on American television; 49 per cent of the viewing audience watched its debut and it ran on broadcast every October for fifty-four years before moving to Apple TV+. Halloween (1978) was made for 300,000 to 325,000 dollars, nearly a quarter of it on Panavision cameras so it would look expensive; it opened softly, then doubled, tripled and ten-timed its takings in the following weeks and ended near 70 million. Michael Jackson's Thriller (2 December 1983) is a fourteen-minute film by John Landis for about half a million dollars; the making-of sold nine million tapes and the film was the first music video admitted to the National Film Registry. Hocus Pocus (16 July 1993) flopped: 28 million budget, 39.5 million gross, fourth behind Free Willy, 39 per cent on Rotten Tomatoes, a loss to Disney of about 16.5 million, until ABC Family put it in its October block in the early 2000s and, in screenwriter Mick Garris's words, "each year the ratings would go up whenever they would air it." Freeform now shows it twenty times in the month, twice on Halloween night. The Nightmare Before Christmas (October 1993) began as a three-page poem Tim Burton wrote at Disney in 1982, imagined as read by Vincent Price; Henry Selick's crew started shooting with no script, only the poem and three Danny Elfman songs. Over the Garden Wall (Cartoon Network, 3 to 7 November 2014) is ten chapters of eleven minutes, won the Emmy for Outstanding Animated Program, and has become something people rewatch, in Entertainment Weekly's words, "the moment the leaves start to change."

## Why rewatching is its own pleasure
Cristel Russell and Sidney Levy's 2012 study in the Journal of Consumer Research, "The Temporal and Focal Dynamics of Volitional Reconsumption," interviewed people about the books, films and places they return to on purpose. Their finding: re-experience is not a lesser version of first experience. Because the plot is known, attention moves to detail, texture and the self; the viewer notices what they missed and measures who they are now against who they were last time. They distinguish regressive reconsumption, going back to a past feeling, from progressive, using the familiar thing to think forward. A 2025 review found that over 95 per cent of more than a thousand respondents deliberately rewatch, chiefly for comfort and emotion regulation, for connection with the people they watched it with, and for continuity of identity. The consequence for a maker: the first viewing has to earn the second, but the second viewing is the product.

Jaye Derrick's 2012 paper in Social Psychological and Personality Science found that people who had spent their self-control on a hard task and then wrote about a favourite television show recovered willpower that a neutral task did not; her term is the social surrogate, a known story whose people are company. Nicholas Rogers's Halloween: From Pagan Ritual to Party Night (Oxford, 2002) argues the night outlived the taming of other holidays because it kept "a public space for social inversion."

Patrick McHale said this out loud about Over the Garden Wall: "The goal was definitely to make a holiday special that could be rewatched every year. I never had any illusions that it could be a big smash hit, but I did hope that it might become a sort of cult classic." His method: "A lot of decisions were based on the mood that we were trying to evoke rather than story... so story elements are sometimes a little disjointed, but those things make it feel even more like a dream." He wanted the music and the pictures to make people "nostalgic for things they hadn't necessarily experienced before."

## What the six have in common
A single piece of music that is the thing. Carpenter wrote the Halloween theme in a few days on a piano in 5/4, a rhythm his father had taught him on bongos as a boy; the critic James Berardinelli called it "relatively simple and unsophisticated" and "one of its strongest assets" in the same breath. Guaraldi's "Linus and Lucy" and "The Great Pumpkin Waltz" do it for Peanuts; Elfman's "This Is Halloween" is a roll-call of monsters sung by the monsters; Rod Temperton wrote Vincent Price's "Thriller" rap in a taxi on the way to the studio and Price did it in two takes; the Blasting Company's parlour songs carry Over the Garden Wall. Each is heard again before anything is seen again, which is how a tune becomes a calendar.

A fixed set of images that fit on a shirt. The white mask, the pumpkin patch, the red leather jacket, Jack's pinstripes and bat bow-tie, Wirt's cone hat and Greg's teapot, the Sanderson sisters' hair. All six build their iconography out of three or four shapes and colours and never dilute them.

Ritual inside the story. Linus goes to the pumpkin patch every year and every year the Great Pumpkin does not come; the special is about a ritual that fails, which is the joke and the ache. Jack Skellington's town does the same Halloween every year until he gets bored; Thriller walks the beats of a 1950s date, the drive-in and the walk home past the graveyard; Over the Garden Wall is a walk through ten stops that each have their own song and their own rule. The audience knows the next stop and leans into it.

Dread let out on a schedule. Halloween is, as one retrospective puts it, "mostly slash-free": the fear is in the watching, the shape across the street in daylight, the laundry on the line, the breathing, and each build is followed by air, a joke from Lynda or a cut to the children. The Great Pumpkin has no horror beyond the title card; Nightmare puts its real menace, Oogie Boogie, in one song near the end; Over the Garden Wall holds the Beast back until chapter ten and shows him as a silhouette with antlers and two lantern eyes. Thriller is the model in miniature: fake-out werewolf, laugh, song, graveyard, dance, chase, wake-up, yellow eyes.

An ending that is not closed. Linus's last line is "Just wait till next year, Charlie Brown. Next year at this same time I'll find a pumpkin patch that is real sincere." Halloween ends with Loomis looking over the balcony at an empty lawn, then the empty rooms with Michael breathing over them. Thriller ends on Michael turning to the camera with his cat's eyes while Price laughs. Nightmare resets the calendar; Hocus Pocus ends on the spellbook opening its eye; Over the Garden Wall ends with a last look back into the Unknown, its people going on without the boys, leaving open whether any of it happened. None says "the end." Each says "next year."

## More classics, and what their makers actually did
Halloween (1978). Nick Castle, credited as The Shape, asked Carpenter what the character would do and was told, "Just walk across the street"; the head tilt over the pinned body was called mid-take, "tilt your head to the right, now tilt your head to the left," and Castle only saw in the cut that "it looks like I'm admiring my kill." To reveal the face Dean Cundey hid a lamp out of shot and faded it up until the mask loomed out of the dark. The monster does less than anyone else in the frame.

Nosferatu (1922). The shadow climbing the stairs to Ellen's room shows the shadow and not the figure, so Orlok's fingers become claws; Gilberto Perez in Sight and Sound follows it into her bedroom as a figure of its own.

The Universal monsters. Jack Pierce rebuilt Karloff's Frankenstein brow every morning in about four hours from cotton glued into spirit gum and sealed with collodion; the skin, Spooky Isles notes, was grey, not green, for black-and-white stock. In Dracula (1931) Karl Freund lit Lugosi's eyes with pinpoint lights.

Suspiria (1977). Luciano Tovoli: "I decided to intensively utilize primary colors, blue, green and red, to identify the normal flow of life, and then apply a complementary color, mainly yellow, to contaminate them." He put coloured fabric over carbon-arc lamps instead of gels; Argento's target was the colour of Disney's Snow White.

Sleepy Hollow (1999). Rick Heinrichs and Peter Young won the Art Direction Oscar. Burton first wanted black-and-white, so Emmanuel Lubezki shot through Deluxe's silver-retention process, "really muted colors, but an incredible amount of contrast," Katrina's room the one red, smoke used heavily and kept high.

The Witch (2015). Robert Eggers read possession accounts and the diaries of Samuel Sewall and John Winthrop, copied phrases into a phrase book sorted by situation and wrote the dialogue from it. Linda Muir worked from thirty-five volumes on common people's clothing, the garments hand-stitched "like clothing, not costumes," and Jarin Blaschke lit interiors by window light alone: "I would have felt I was betraying that with movie lights."

Trick 'r Treat (2007). Four interlocking stories on one night in Warren Valley, Ohio, told out of order; Sam, in orange footie pyjamas and a burlap sack, enforces four rules: always wear a costume, always give treats, never blow out a jack-o'-lantern before midnight, always check your candy.

Treehouse of Horror (since 1990). Bill Oakley's formula: a warning or wraparound, then three one-act segments, and "You were allowed to go WAY outside the bounds of what we normally were allowed to do."

The War of the Worlds (CBS, 30 October 1938, 8 p.m.). Dance music; a first bulletin about gas on Mars within the opening minutes, more music, a calm Princeton astronomer played by Welles, reporters each worse than the last, and by the forty-minute mark machines gassing Manhattan. Welles closed out of character: "if your doorbell rings and nobody's there, that was no Martian, it's Halloween!"

Coraline (2009). Henry Selick photographed his three-year-old son lying on the floor with buttons over his eyes: "It was terrifying." Buttons "dehumanize," make a face "almost insect-like," "turned into a living doll."

## What the research on ritual says
Brooks, Norton and colleagues (Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2016) had people sing Journey to strangers; those who first drew their feelings, sprinkled salt on the drawing, counted aloud and threw it away had lower heart rates, less anxiety and sang better, and calling the actions a "ritual" beat calling them "random behaviors." Konvalinka, Xygalatas and others (PNAS, 2011) fitted heart monitors to a Spanish fire-walk: the twelve walkers and the nine spectators who were their kin rose and fell together; seventeen visiting strangers did not.

## Structure patterns
The cold open exists, in Wikipedia's account, to involve the audience before the first break so they do not change channel.

The rule stated, then broken. Gremlins (1984) gives Billy three rules in its first act, no light, no water, never feed it after midnight, and the plot is their breaking in order, water by a careless friend, midnight by a tampered clock. Trick 'r Treat's four rules each cost the one who breaks them.

Suspense, not surprise. Hitchcock to Truffaut: a bomb under the table that goes off unannounced buys fifteen seconds of surprise; a bomb the audience has seen, with the clock at a quarter to one, buys fifteen minutes of suspense.

The ending that names the night: Welles's "it's Halloween!", Linus's next year, the spellbook's eye.

## Sound
The Halloween theme is in 5/4 with the accents grouped 3, 3, 2, 2; Carpenter wrote the score in three days and recorded it at Sound Arts in Los Angeles. Thriller runs about 118 beats per minute in Music Gateway's analysis. "This Is Halloween" is in a minor key, 4/4 with alternating beats accented, and, as Classic FM notes, slips into 3/4 at the end of the chorus.

## Visual language for a witch's ritual on screen
Candles. Tenebrae, the Holy Week office: fifteen candles on a stand called a hearse, one put out after each psalm, the last hidden rather than snuffed so the church sits in the dark, then the strepitus, a great noise of slammed books. A Wiccan altar, as Wikipedia lays it out, is quartered by element: earth north (pentacle or salt), air east (incense), fire south (candle), water west (chalice), the Goddess candle left, the God candle right.

Sigils. Austin Osman Spare's method: write the desire as a sentence, strike every repeated letter, fuse the remaining letters into one glyph, charge it in trance or exhaustion, then forget it.

Smoke. Haze reads as texture; fog is denser and dissipates faster, and it takes the colour of the light through it, so purple smoke is purple light in white smoke.

Costume. History.com: through most of the seventeenth century women of every class wore tall black wool-felt hats, the wide-brimmed conical style peaking in the mid-1600s, and Katherine Walker ties the black to the Renaissance link between the colour and the devil. Mari Ellis Dunning (The Conversation, 14 October 2025): the earliest picture of a witch on a broomstick is in the 1485 manuscript of Martin Le Franc's Le Champion des Dames.

## Holding a narrator-led ten to fifteen minutes by the minute
YouTube's retention report defines Intro as the share still watching after 30 seconds; dips mark where viewers leave or skip. vidIQ's guidance: keep at least 60 per cent past 30 seconds, and the viewer should know what they are getting within about 15 seconds. Outlierkit reads a cliff in the first 30 seconds as a hook problem and a slow slide through the middle as pacing, and adds that talking-head videos without visual change lose people around minutes three to five. AIR Media-Tech's audit of a channel at 16 to 23 per cent viewed on videos over ten minutes rose to 38 after chapters, short recaps and visual breaks went in together, though chapters invite skipping in narrative video.

## Rules for a ten-to-fifteen-minute cinematic witch video with a herbalist narrator
One theme, under a minute, heard in the cold open before her face and again under the last shot; the same recording every year, with a lurch in it, as Carpenter's 5/4.

Open on the ritual, not the lecture. Her hands doing the thing she does every 31 October, candle, bowl, knife, plant, before a word of history. The viewer should be able to describe the opening in one sentence a year later, and know what the video is within 15 seconds.

Three or four fixed images that are hers alone: a specific lantern, a specific jar, a specific room, a specific shape at the window. Repeat them; never replace them with something prettier next year.

Structure as a walk with stops, in the manner of Over the Garden Wall and the Danse Macabre: a chapter per plant or per horror, each with its name on screen, its own dread and its own release, five or six in fifteen minutes, so the video can be remembered as a list, a new room every two to three minutes.

State a rule in the first minute, in plain words, and break it in the last third.

Dread by withholding. Show the effect before the thing: the lamed pedlar before the witch, the swollen grave before the corpse, the bitten man before the serpent. Keep the single worst image for the last third and show it once. Tell the viewer where the bomb is and show the clock.

Her face lit up out of the dark rather than cut to; the shadow of the hand rather than the hand; candles put out on a schedule, the last hidden not snuffed, the loud noise in the dark; one palette for ordinary life and one colour that invades it.

Release after each peak, in her voice: a dry remark, a practical instruction, the plant's real name on screen. She is the babysitter and the doctor at once; the audience trusts the one who is not afraid.

Legend and record told as different things, in the sentence. "The story goes" is a pacing tool: it lets her tell the blood bath and then take it away, which is a second scare. Where the period can speak, let it.

End on an invitation and a loop: the candle snuffed, the same theme, her line that names the night and next year. The frame the video opens on should be the frame it ends on, one year older.
