# The Craft of a Halloween Classic: What Gets Watched Every Year

## The six that people actually return to
It's the Great Pumpkin, Charlie Brown (CBS, 27 October 1966) was the first Halloween special on American television; 49 per cent of the viewing audience watched its debut and it ran on broadcast every October for fifty-four years before moving to Apple TV+. Halloween (1978) was made for 300,000 to 325,000 dollars, nearly a quarter of it on Panavision cameras so it would look expensive; it opened softly, then doubled, tripled and ten-timed its takings in the following weeks and ended near 70 million. Michael Jackson's Thriller (2 December 1983) is a fourteen-minute film by John Landis for about half a million dollars; the making-of sold nine million tapes and the film was the first music video admitted to the National Film Registry. Hocus Pocus (16 July 1993) flopped: 28 million budget, 39.5 million gross, fourth behind Free Willy, 39 per cent on Rotten Tomatoes, a loss to Disney of about 16.5 million, until ABC Family put it in its October block in the early 2000s and, in screenwriter Mick Garris's words, "each year the ratings would go up whenever they would air it." Freeform now shows it twenty times in the month, twice on Halloween night. The Nightmare Before Christmas (October 1993) began as a three-page poem Tim Burton wrote at Disney in 1982, imagined as read by Vincent Price; Henry Selick's crew started shooting with no script, only the poem and three Danny Elfman songs. Over the Garden Wall (Cartoon Network, 3 to 7 November 2014) is ten chapters of eleven minutes, won the Emmy for Outstanding Animated Program, and has become something people rewatch, in Entertainment Weekly's words, "the moment the leaves start to change."

## Why rewatching is its own pleasure
Cristel Russell and Sidney Levy's 2012 study in the Journal of Consumer Research, "The Temporal and Focal Dynamics of Volitional Reconsumption," interviewed people about the books, films and places they return to on purpose. Their finding: re-experience is not a lesser version of first experience. Because the plot is known, attention moves to detail, texture and the self; the viewer notices what they missed and measures who they are now against who they were last time. They distinguish regressive reconsumption, going back to a past feeling, from progressive, using the familiar thing to think forward. A 2025 review found that over 95 per cent of more than a thousand respondents deliberately rewatch, chiefly for comfort and emotion regulation, for connection with the people they watched it with, and for continuity of identity. The consequence for a maker: the first viewing has to earn the second, but the second viewing is the product.

Patrick McHale said this out loud about Over the Garden Wall: "The goal was definitely to make a holiday special that could be rewatched every year. I never had any illusions that it could be a big smash hit, but I did hope that it might become a sort of cult classic." His method: "A lot of decisions were based on the mood that we were trying to evoke rather than story... so story elements are sometimes a little disjointed, but those things make it feel even more like a dream." He wanted the music and the pictures to make people "nostalgic for things they hadn't necessarily experienced before."

## What the six have in common
A single piece of music that is the thing. Carpenter wrote the Halloween theme in a few days on a piano in 5/4, a rhythm his father had taught him on bongos as a boy; the critic James Berardinelli called it "relatively simple and unsophisticated" and "one of its strongest assets" in the same breath. Guaraldi's "Linus and Lucy" and "The Great Pumpkin Waltz" do it for Peanuts; Elfman's "This Is Halloween" is a roll-call of monsters sung by the monsters; Rod Temperton wrote Vincent Price's "Thriller" rap in a taxi on the way to the studio and Price did it in two takes; the Blasting Company's parlour songs carry Over the Garden Wall. Each is heard again before anything is seen again, which is how a tune becomes a calendar.

A fixed set of images that fit on a shirt. The white mask, the pumpkin patch, the red leather jacket, Jack's pinstripes and bat bow-tie, Wirt's cone hat and Greg's teapot, the Sanderson sisters' hair. All six build their iconography out of three or four shapes and colours and never dilute them.

Ritual inside the story. Linus goes to the pumpkin patch every year and every year the Great Pumpkin does not come; the special is about a ritual that fails, which is the joke and the ache. Jack Skellington's town does the same Halloween every year until he gets bored; Thriller walks the beats of a 1950s date, the drive-in and the walk home past the graveyard; Over the Garden Wall is a walk through ten stops that each have their own song and their own rule. The audience knows the next stop and leans into it.

Dread let out on a schedule. Halloween is, as one retrospective puts it, "mostly slash-free": the fear is in the watching, the shape across the street in daylight, the laundry on the line, the breathing, and each build is followed by air, a joke from Lynda or a cut to the children. The Great Pumpkin has no horror beyond the title card; Nightmare puts its real menace, Oogie Boogie, in one song near the end; Over the Garden Wall holds the Beast back until chapter ten and shows him as a silhouette with antlers and two lantern eyes. Thriller is the model in miniature: fake-out werewolf, laugh, song, graveyard, dance, chase, wake-up, yellow eyes.

An ending that is not closed. Linus's last line is "Just wait till next year, Charlie Brown. Next year at this same time I'll find a pumpkin patch that is real sincere." Halloween ends with Loomis looking over the balcony at an empty lawn, then the empty rooms with Michael breathing over them. Thriller ends on Michael turning to the camera with his cat's eyes while Price laughs. Nightmare resets the calendar; Hocus Pocus ends on the spellbook opening its eye; Over the Garden Wall ends with a last look back into the Unknown, its people going on without the boys, leaving open whether any of it happened. None says "the end." Each says "next year."

## Rules for a ten-to-fifteen-minute cinematic witch video with a herbalist narrator
One theme, under a minute, heard in the cold open before her face and again under the last shot; the same recording every year.

Open on the ritual, not the lecture. Her hands doing the thing she does every 31 October, candle, bowl, knife, plant, before a word of history. The viewer should be able to describe the opening in one sentence a year later.

Three or four fixed images that are hers alone: a specific lantern, a specific jar, a specific room, a specific shape at the window. Repeat them; never replace them with something prettier next year.

Structure as a walk with stops, in the manner of Over the Garden Wall and the Danse Macabre: a chapter per plant or per horror, each with its name on screen, its own dread and its own release, five or six in fifteen minutes, so the video can be remembered as a list.

Dread by withholding. Show the effect before the thing: the lamed pedlar before the witch, the swollen grave before the corpse, the bitten man before the serpent. Keep the single worst image for the last third and show it once.

Release after each peak, in her voice: a dry remark, a practical instruction, the plant's real name on screen. She is the babysitter and the doctor at once; the audience trusts the one who is not afraid.

Legend and record told as different things, in the sentence. "The story goes" is a pacing tool: it lets her tell the blood bath and then take it away, which is a second scare.

End on an invitation and a loop: the candle snuffed, the same theme, her line that names next year. The frame the video opens on should be the frame it ends on, one year older.
