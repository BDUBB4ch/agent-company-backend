# Gore, Blood and the Body in Horror Folklore: Legend Against Record

## Elizabeth Báthory: the legend and the trial record
The legend: a Hungarian countess who bathed in the blood of six hundred virgins to stay young. The record: in 1610 the Palatine György Thurzó, her cousin, collected 289 witness statements, more than 250 of them hearsay or nothing at all. On the night of 29 December 1610 his men entered Csejte; by his own letter to his wife they found one girl dead and one dying. The dead girl, Doricza, had been beaten over days with a club, and the Latin of the 1611 record says she was "ex flagris et torturis miserabiliter extinctam," miserably killed by whippings and tortures.

Four of her household were tried at Bytča on 2 and 7 January 1611, after torture: Ilona Jó, Dorottya Szentes, Katalin Benická and the manservant János Újváry, called Ficzkó. Asked how many girls had died, each gave a different count: Szentes 36, Ficzkó 37, Benická 50, Jó 51. Thurzó charged eighty. What they described was torture, not bathing: girls beaten with cudgels until, one witness said, the skin was "black as charcoal"; needles under the fingernails and through the lips; flesh cut from the body and burned; red-hot irons to the face; girls stripped, stood in the snow and doused with water until they froze; starvation in the dungeons; nettles in open wounds. The number 650 comes from one servant girl, Zsuzsanna, who said an official named Szilvássy had seen it in one of the countess's books; the book was never produced and Szilvássy never mentioned it. Szentes and Jó had their fingers torn out with red-hot pincers and were burned alive; Ficzkó was beheaded and burned. The countess was never tried, was confined at Csejte, and died on 21 August 1614. The blood bath is in no document from her lifetime; it first appears in print in 1729 in László Turóczi's Tragica Historia, by a Jesuit who had never seen the sealed trial papers.

## Sawney Bean and Christie Cleek
The Newgate Calendar tells of Alexander "Sawney" Bean, who with his wife bred eight sons, six daughters, eighteen grandsons and fourteen granddaughters in a sea cave on the Galloway coast, forty-eight people living on travellers they ambushed, butchered, pickled in brine and hung in strips from the cave roof, "upwards of twenty-five years," a thousand victims, until the king came with four hundred men and bloodhounds. The tale first appears in print in London in 1734, in Captain Charles Johnson's history of highwaymen; Johnson gave no source, and the earliest dated Scottish chapbooks are from 1775. There is no court record, no chronicle, no name in any register of any reign the story has been set in. Historian Louise Yeoman notes it was published in England after the Jacobite risings as a picture of the savage Scot. It is legend.

Scotland has an older cannibal with a record. Andrew of Wyntoun's Orygynale Cronykil, written around 1420, names "Chwsten Cleek," who in the famine years around 1339-41 caught "children and women" and "ate them up." Folklore holds that he was Andrew Christie, a Perth butcher, and that his band hid in a Grampian pass and pulled riders from their horses with an iron hook on a pole, the "cleke"; the chronicles record only the cannibalism and the trapping.

## Vlad III and the forest of stakes
Vlad III Dracula ruled Wallachia three times between 1448 and 1476. In the winter of 1461-62 he raided Ottoman Bulgaria and on 11 February 1462 wrote to King Matthias Corvinus: "We killed 23,884 Turks and Bulgarians without counting those whom we burned in their homes or whose heads were not cut by our soldiers," and sent sacks of noses and ears as proof. In June 1462 Mehmed II marched on him; on the night of 17 June Vlad rode into the Ottoman camp with torches trying to kill the Sultan in his tent. On the approaches to Târgoviște the Ottomans found, in the words of the Byzantine historian Chalkokondyles, a field of stakes with twenty thousand bodies on them, men, women and children, the envoy Hamza Pasha on the tallest, and birds nesting in the bodies. The Sultan turned back. Chalkokondyles is the only source for the twenty thousand. The Ottoman chronicler Enveri, who marched that year, says he saw a field of impaled Turks but gives no number; Tursun Beg's official history of the campaign never mentions the forest. Peter Mario Kreuter calls it "an exaggerated literary episode." The impalement itself is not in doubt: the stake was oiled, inserted through the anus or the vagina and driven up the body so as to miss the heart, and the victim could live for days.

The German tradition begins with Michael Beheim's poem of about 1463, "Of a tyrant called Dracula, voivode of Wallachia"; it has him dining among the impaled after the Brașov raid of St Bartholomew's Day 1459. The pamphlet Die Geschichte Dracole Waide was printed in Nuremberg on 14 October 1488 and pirated within months; thirteen editions are known to 1568, and the picture of Vlad at table among the stakes fronts Markus Ayrer's 1499 Nuremberg edition and Matthias Hupfuff's 1500 Strasbourg one. The Russian Skazanie o Drakule voevode was composed between 1481 and 1486, most likely by Fyodor Kuritsyn, Ivan III's ambassador to Buda. Its nineteen anecdotes include envoys who kept their hats on, so "he ordered that their hats should be nailed to their heads with small iron nails"; a golden cup left at a public spring that no one dared steal; beggars and the sick feasted in a hall and burned in it.

## Gilles de Rais
A marshal of France who had fought beside Joan of Arc, Gilles de Rais was summoned on 13 September 1440 for "murdering children, sodomy, invoking demons, offending the Divine Majesty and heresy," and tried at Nantes before the bishop's court and then the duke's. On 21 October, told that torture would follow, he asked to confess first. The record of 22 October has him admit "each and every one of the things contained in the articles," and that he "could not determine with certitude the number" he had killed and caused to be killed. He described children brought to his rooms at Machecoul, Tiffauges and Champtocé, throats cut or heads taken off with a short sword, bodies opened, and himself sitting on the bellies of the dying to watch them go; he held up the heads and limbs he found finest and admired them, and the bodies went into the fireplace of his chamber with the help of Étienne Corrillaut, called Poitou, and Henriet Griart, who confessed on 23 October without torture. Henriet said the bones of about forty children were carried out of a tower at Machecoul and burned; the indictment counts forty-five heads, the ashes thrown "into secret and out-of-the-way places." None of the parents who testified had seen a child taken, and no body was produced in court. He was sentenced on 25 October and at nine the next morning, 26 October 1440, hanged beside Poitou and Henriet above a pyre at Nantes.

## The dancing plague of Strasbourg, 1518
On 14 July 1518 a woman the chronicles call Frau Troffea stepped into a Strasbourg street and danced. She danced for four to six days, collapsing and getting up again, no music, until her feet were swollen and bleeding through her shoes. Within a month the Imlin family chronicle counted four hundred. The council, told by physicians this was "hot blood," decided the cure was more dancing: Daniel Specklin's chronicle says they opened the halls, built a stage, hired musicians and strong men to hold the dancers up, and that "many danced themselves to death." One later chronicle claimed fifteen a day were dying of heart attacks, strokes and exhaustion; the surviving city records give no clean toll, and the historian John Waller puts the dead in the dozens. In late August the dancers were carted to the shrine of St Vitus at Saverne, given small crosses to hold and red shoes sprinkled with holy water, and it stopped. Waller's explanation is mass psychogenic illness after famine, smallpox and syphilis.

## Ergotism: St Anthony's fire
The fungus Claviceps purpurea grows on rye in wet years as dark purple spurs among the grain, and when they are milled into the bread the alkaloids go with them. In the gangrenous form the blood vessels clamp shut: a burning in the fingers and toes, the "holy fire," then the limbs go cold and numb, blacken, dry and mummify, and hands and feet drop off at the joint without bleeding; sufferers lived on as stumps. In the convulsive form there are cramps, seizures, crawling and itching under the skin, vomiting, mania and visions.

The monk Raoul Glaber, writing of the Aquitaine epidemic of 994, called it the "hidden fire," a burning that consumed a limb and separated it from the body, often in a single night. Sigebert of Gembloux wrote of 1089 in Lorraine that the sickness "caused their limbs to become as black as coal," so that hands and feet fell away. Out of that outbreak came the cure: a nobleman named Gaston, whose son recovered, founded in 1095 at La Motte-Saint-Didier the Hospital Brothers of St Anthony. The Antonines fed their patients bread baked without rye and "saint vinage," wine in which the relics had been steeped, and the patients got better because they had stopped eating the poison.

The last big European outbreak was at Pont-Saint-Esprit in the Gard in August 1951. From one baker's bread more than 250 people were sick, around 50 ended in asylums, seven died. Gabbai, Lisbonne and Pourquier reported the cases in the British Medical Journal on 15 September 1951: "gusts of warmth" followed by "cold waves," drenching sweats, "burning sensations throughout the whole of the digestive tract"; the mental symptoms, they wrote, "called to mind the old name of the disease, Mal des Ardents." One man screamed that snakes were eating him from inside; another leapt from a third-floor window convinced he could fly. A 2009 claim that the CIA dosed the town with LSD has no document behind it.

In 1976 Linnda Caporael argued in Science that the Salem girls' fits, visions and sensations of pinching were convulsive ergotism from the 1691 rye. Nicholas Spanos and Jack Gottlieb replied in the same journal: convulsive ergotism occurs where vitamin A is lacking, and Salem ate fish; there was no epidemic of convulsions among the village's small children; and the afflicted were well outside the courtroom and saw named neighbours, not drug visions. Most historians side with Spanos.

## Scalps: the price list
Scalping was paid for by the state. On 17 November 1724 the Massachusetts General Court set 100 pounds for each male scalp. Captain John Lovewell's company found a hunting camp on 20 February 1725, killed all ten, and marched into Boston in early March to collect 1,000 pounds; the scalps were paraded through the streets. He was killed at Pequawket on 8 May 1725; Lieutenant Seth Wyman took 100 pounds for the scalp of the sachem Paugus. On 3 November 1755 Lieutenant Governor Spencer Phips of Massachusetts Bay declared the Penobscot "Enemies, Rebels and Traitors to his Majesty King George the Second" and set the prices: fifty pounds for a male Penobscot over twelve brought to Boston alive, forty pounds "for every Scalp of a Male Penobscot Indian above the Age aforesaid, brought in as Evidence of their being killed," twenty-five pounds for a woman or a boy under twelve taken alive, twenty pounds for their scalps.

## Hands, ears, bowels and bones: the law's own gore
In 1579 the Puritan lawyer John Stubbs published a pamphlet against Queen Elizabeth's marriage to the Duke of Anjou and was sentenced under a statute of Philip and Mary to lose his right hand. On 3 November 1579, in the market place at Westminster, with surgeons standing by, the hand was struck off, in William Camden's words, "with a cleaver driven through the wrist by the force of a mallet"; an eyewitness said it took three blows. Before the blow Stubbs said, "Pray for me now my calamity is at hand." After it he pulled off his hat with his left hand, cried "God save the Queen," and fainted; the stump was seared with a hot iron. The statutes made this ordinary: the forgery act of 1563 had the offender stand in the pillory and "have both his ears cut off, and his nostrils slit, and seared," the marks "to remain as a perpetual mark of his falsehood."

On 30 June 1637 the Star Chamber's sentence on William Prynne, Henry Burton and John Bastwick was carried out on two pillories in Palace Yard. Nehemiah Wallington watched: Prynne went up first, his wife "saluted each ear with a kiss," and he told the hangman, "Come, friend, come, burn me, cut me, I fear it not." Prynne had lost his ears once already, in 1634, so the executioner sawed off the stumps and burned S.L., seditious libeller, into both cheeks. Bastwick brought his own scalpel; his wife kissed his ears and then, a letter from George Garrard to Lord Strafford says, took them home in a handkerchief.

Samuel Pepys watched the full ceremony. On Saturday 13 October 1660 he went to Charing Cross "to see Major-General Harrison hanged, drawn, and quartered; which was done there, he looking as cheerful as any man could do in that condition. He was presently cut down, and his head and heart shown to the people, at which there was great shouts of joy." The sentence read that he be hanged, "cut down" alive, and his entrails "taken out of your body and, you living, the same to be burnt before your eyes."

On the continent men were broken on the wheel: the long bones of arms and legs shattered with an iron bar, the limbs threaded through the spokes of a cart wheel raised on a post, the man left facing the sky. In Toulouse on 10 March 1762 the Protestant Jean Calas, sixty-four, convicted of murdering his son, was tied over the wheel face up before the cathedral, his limbs broken, and left two hours before the executioner strangled him. In Paris on 28 March 1757 Robert-François Damiens, who had scratched Louis XV with a penknife, had the hand that held the knife burned in sulphur, his flesh torn with red-hot pincers, "molten lead, boiling oil, burning pitch, wax and sulphur melted together" poured into the wounds, and his body pulled apart by horses in the Place de Grève. It took most of a day; four horses and then six could not do it, and the executioner cut the joints.

## Mummy on the shelf: corpse medicine
European apothecaries sold human remains as drugs. In 1564 the physician Guy de la Fontaine went to Alexandria to see a dealer's stock; the man showed him bodies "piled one upon another" and admitted they were the recent dead, slaves and beggars, gutted, filled with pitch and dried. Pierre Pomet, chief druggist to Louis XIV, told buyers in 1694 to choose mummy "of a fine shining black, free of bones and dirt, with a good smell,"

The English court drank skull. Jonathan Goddard, Cromwell's physician, made his drops from five pounds of the skull of a man hanged or dead by violence, two pounds of dried vipers, two of hartshorn and two of ivory, distilled; Charles II bought the recipe, reportedly for 6,000 pounds, and it became the King's Drops. On 2 February 1685, the morning his last illness began, it was the first thing poured into him, forty drops at a time, until he died four days later. Executioners in the German lands sold the fat of the people they killed as Armsünderfett, "poor sinner's fat," for ointments.

## Surgery without anaesthesia: the surgeons' own books
William Clowes in A Prooved Practise for All Young Chirurgeons of 1588 set out the amputation plainly: a strong table, assistants astride the patient to hold him down, a tight binding above the cut because "the pain of the binding doth greatly obscure the knife and feeling of the incision," the knife round to the bone, the saw, the vessels taken up or the stump seared. John Woodall's The Surgions Mate of 1617 gives the same counsel: "Let him prepare his soule as a ready sacrifice to the Lord by earnest praiers, craving mercie and help from the Almighty."

On 26 March 1658 the lithotomist Thomas Hollier cut him for a bladder stone at his cousin's house in Salisbury Court. He was tied down and held by four strong men; a silver probe went up the urethra to steady the stone, the knife went in through the perineum about three inches, the stone was found and dragged out with pincers, and the wound was left open to drain. The stone was the size of a tennis ball; Pepys kept it in a case and held a dinner on the anniversary every year he could.

## The Grimms before the nursery
The first volume of the Kinder- und Hausmärchen came out in 1812 with 86 tales and the second in 1815 with 70, and every later edition was softer. Tale 22 of 1812, "How the Children Played Butcher with Each Other," is two stories. In the first, children of five and six play butcher, cook and pig, and the "butcher" cuts the "pig's" throat; the court, unsure whether to hang a child, offers him a red apple in one hand and a Rhenish gulden in the other, and when the boy takes the apple he is let go as innocent. In the second a boy watches his father kill a pig, does the same to his little brother, and his mother, running from the bath, stabs him with the same knife; the baby drowns in the tub, she hangs herself, and the father dies of grief. The brothers dropped it from the 1819 edition. In the 1812 Snow White the queen is the girl's own mother, and the mother orders the huntsman to bring back the lungs and liver so she can eat them; the stepmother arrives in 1819. In the 1812 Cinderella one sister cuts off her toe and the other her heel to get the shoe on, and the doves sing that there is blood in the shoe; the doves that peck out the sisters' eyes are an 1819 addition. The Juniper Tree has the stepmother behead the boy with the lid of the apple chest, prop the head back on with a scarf so his sister thinks she did it, and cook him in a stew the father eats and praises; it ends with the bird's song, "my mother she killed me, my father he ate me, my sister Marlene gathered my bones," and a millstone dropped on the stepmother's head.
