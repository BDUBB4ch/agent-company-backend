# Gore, Blood and the Body in Horror Folklore: Legend Against Record

## Elizabeth Báthory: the legend and the trial record
The legend: a Hungarian countess who bathed in the blood of six hundred virgins to stay young. The record: in 1610 the Palatine György Thurzó, her cousin, collected 289 witness statements, of which more than 250 were hearsay or nothing at all. Four of her household were tried at Bytča on 2 and 7 January 1611, after torture: Ilona Jó, Dorottya Szentes, Katalin Beneczky and the manservant János Újváry, called Ficzkó.

What they described was torture, not bathing. Girls beaten with cudgels until, one witness said, the skin was "black as charcoal"; needles pushed under the fingernails and through the lips; flesh cut from the body and burned; red-hot irons and tongs to the face; girls stripped and stood in the snow and doused with water until they froze; starvation in the dungeons; stinging nettles pushed into open wounds. The accomplices admitted to burying between thirty-six and fifty-one; Thurzó charged eighty. The number 650 comes from one servant girl, Zsuzsanna, who said a court official had seen it in one of the countess's books; the book was never produced. Szentes and Jó had their fingers torn out with red-hot pincers and were burned alive; Ficzkó was beheaded. The countess was never tried, was walled into rooms at Csejte, and died on 21 August 1614. The blood bath is in no document from her lifetime. It first appears in 1729 in László Turóczi's Tragica Historia, written by a Jesuit who had never seen the sealed trial papers, 115 years after her death.

## Sawney Bean: a story with no record at all
The Newgate Calendar, in its eighteenth-century editions, tells of Alexander "Sawney" Bean, who with his wife bred eight sons, six daughters, eighteen grandsons and fourteen granddaughters in a sea cave on the Galloway coast, forty-eight people living on travellers they ambushed, butchered, pickled in brine and hung in strips from the cave roof, "upwards of twenty-five years," a thousand victims, until the king came with four hundred men and bloodhounds. There is no court record, no chronicle, no name in any register of any reign the story has been set in. Historian Louise Yeoman notes the tale appears only after the Jacobite risings, in London pamphlets, as a picture of the savage Scot. It is legend.

## Vlad III and the forest of stakes
Vlad III Dracula ruled Wallachia three times between 1448 and 1476. In the winter of 1461-62 he raided Ottoman Bulgaria and reported to the Hungarian king, with sacks of noses and ears as proof, 23,884 killed. In June 1462 Mehmed II marched on him; on the night of 17 June Vlad rode into the Ottoman camp with torches trying to kill the Sultan in his tent. When the Ottomans reached the approaches to Târgoviște they found, in the words of the Byzantine historian Chalkokondyles, a field of stakes with twenty thousand bodies on them, men, women and children, the envoy Hamza Pasha on the tallest, and birds, the chronicler says, nesting in the bodies. The Sultan turned back. Chalkokondyles is the only source for the twenty thousand, and the historian Peter Mario Kreuter calls the forest "an exaggerated literary episode." The impalement itself is not in doubt: the stake was oiled, inserted through the anus or the vagina and driven up the body so as to miss the heart, and the victim could live for days.

The German printing trade made him a bestseller. Peter Wagner's 1488 Nuremberg Dracole Waida has a portrait on its title page; the famous image of Vlad dining at a table among the impaled is the frontispiece of Markus Ayrer's 1499 Nuremberg edition and Matthias Hupfuff's 1500 Strasbourg one.

## Gilles de Rais
A marshal of France who had fought beside Joan of Arc, Gilles de Rais was summoned on 13 September 1440 for "murdering children, sodomy, invoking demons, offending the Divine Majesty and heresy," arrested two days later and tried at Nantes before the bishop's court and then the duke's. On 21 October, facing torture, he confessed to the murders of at least 140 children, mostly boys, taken from the villages round his castles by his servants, and described cutting their throats, severing heads, opening bodies and sitting on the dying to watch them go. His accomplices Henriet and Poitou confessed in the same detail. He was sentenced on 25 October and at nine the next morning, 26 October 1440, hanged and burned beside them. None of the parents who testified had seen a child taken.

## The dancing plague of Strasbourg, 1518
On 14 July 1518 a woman the chronicles call Frau Troffea stepped into a Strasbourg street and danced. She danced for four to six days, collapsing and getting up again, no music, until her feet were swollen and bleeding through her shoes. Within a month the Imlin family chronicle counted four hundred dancers. The council, told by physicians this was "hot blood," decided the cure was more dancing: they opened the guild halls, built a stage and hired musicians and strong men to hold the dancers up. One later chronicle claimed fifteen a day were dying of heart attacks, strokes and exhaustion; the surviving city records give no clean toll, and the historian John Waller puts the dead in the dozens. In late August the dancers were carted to the shrine of St Vitus at Saverne, given red shoes and holy water, and it stopped. Waller's explanation is mass psychogenic illness after famine, smallpox and syphilis.

## Ergotism: St Anthony's fire
The fungus Claviceps purpurea grows on rye in wet years as dark purple spurs among the grain, and when they are milled into the bread the alkaloids go with them. In the gangrenous form the blood vessels clamp shut: a burning in the fingers and toes, the "holy fire," then the limbs go cold and numb, blacken, dry and mummify, and hands and feet drop off at the joint without bleeding; sufferers lived on as stumps. In the convulsive form there are cramps, seizures, crawling and itching under the skin, vomiting, mania and visions. The Hospital Brothers of St Anthony, founded near Vienne around 1095, took in the victims. The last big European outbreak was at Pont-Saint-Esprit in France in August 1951: 250 sick from one baker's bread, seven dead, one man screaming that snakes were eating him from inside, another leaping from a third-floor window convinced he could fly.

In 1976 Linnda Caporael argued in Science that the Salem girls' fits, visions and sensations of pinching were convulsive ergotism from the 1691 rye. Nicholas Spanos and Jack Gottlieb replied in the same journal: convulsive ergotism occurs where vitamin A is lacking, and Salem ate fish; there was no epidemic of convulsions among the village's small children; and the afflicted were well outside the courtroom and saw named neighbours, not drug visions. Most historians side with Spanos.

## Scalps and hands: the law's own gore
Scalping was paid for by the state. On 3 November 1755 Lieutenant Governor Spencer Phips of Massachusetts Bay declared the Penobscot "Enemies, Rebels and Traitors to his Majesty King George the Second" and set the prices: fifty pounds for a male Penobscot over twelve brought to Boston alive, forty pounds "for every Scalp of a Male Penobscot Indian above the Age aforesaid, brought in as Evidence of their being killed," twenty-five pounds for a woman or a boy under twelve taken alive, twenty pounds for their scalps.

In 1579 the Puritan lawyer John Stubbs published a pamphlet against Queen Elizabeth's marriage to the Duke of Anjou and was sentenced under a statute of 1554 to lose his right hand. On 3 November 1579, in the market place at Westminster, with surgeons standing by, the hand was struck off, in William Camden's words, "with a cleaver driven through the wrist by the force of a mallet"; an eyewitness said it took three blows. Before the blow Stubbs said, "Pray for me now my calamity is at hand." After it he pulled off his hat with his left hand, cried "God save the Queen," and fainted; the stump was seared with a hot iron.
