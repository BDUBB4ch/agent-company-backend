# Samhain and the Origins of Halloween

## What the oldest Irish texts actually say
The word is Samain (modern Samhain, said roughly SOW-in). A glossary attached to the tale Tochmarc Emire, "The Wooing of Emer", explains it: "Samuin, that is, summer's end." That tale, in its longer Middle Irish version of the eleventh or twelfth century, lists Samain first among the four quarter days with Imbolc, Beltaine and Lughnasa. It was the term-day of a cattle-keeping people: the day the herds came down from summer pasture, rents fell due and the slaughtering for winter began.

Serglige Con Culainn, "Cu Chulainn's Sickbed", says the Ulaid held their Samain assembly for a full week, the day itself and three days either side: feasting, drinking, trading, judging cases, contests. In Echtra Nerai a warrior takes a dare on Samain night, follows a host into the mound at Cruachan and comes back carrying summer flowers to prove where he has been. In the Fenian stories a being called Aillen comes to Tara every Samain, lulls the court asleep with music and burns the hall, until the boy Fionn stays awake and kills him.

What the texts share: at Samain the boundary with the sid, the mounds of the Otherworld, is loosened, and things come out. The Irish Otherworld in these stories is not a land of the dead; it is a bright parallel country of the aos si, the people of the mounds, later softened in English to "fairies". The historian Ronald Hutton, who has gone through every medieval reference, concludes there is no record of a religious ceremony at Samain, no proof it was a festival of the dead, and none that it opened the year. Those ideas came later.

## Where the "Celtic New Year" and the "god Samhain" came from
In 1786 a British military engineer, Charles Vallancey, published a Vindication of the Ancient History of Ireland in which the Irish worshipped a judge of the dead called Saman. No Irish text, annal or manuscript names any such god. The "Celtic New Year" was argued by Sir John Rhys in the 1880s from a nineteenth-century Manx land-tenancy custom and made famous by James Frazer in The Golden Bough; Frazer hedged every sentence with "seems" and "may have," and the retellings dropped the hedges. Keating's history of Ireland, written in the 1630s, is the sole source for the druids' great fire at Tlachtga (the Hill of Ward, County Meath) on Samain eve, from which every hearth was relit. The medieval druidic fire rite in the glossaries belongs to Beltaine, the first of May.

## The Christian date
The earliest solid evidence for All Saints on 1 November is a letter of Alcuin from the year 800: "Kalendis Novembris solemnitas omnium sanctorum." The decree of 835 attributed to Gregory IV is known only from Sigebert of Gembloux in the twelfth century. All Souls, 2 November, the day for praying the dead out of purgatory, followed. No surviving document says the Church chose the date to swallow Samain; the two simply sit on the same three days, and Irish and Scottish custom ran them together as Hallowmas.

## Souling and the soul cake
Praying for the dead was paid in bread. John Mirk's fourteenth-century Festial says "good men and women would this day buy bread and deal it for the souls that they loved, hoping with each loaf to get a soul out of purgatory." Shakespeare has Speed accuse his master of "puling, like a beggar at Hallowmas" (Two Gentlemen of Verona, II.i, 1593). John Aubrey, writing around 1686 to 1697, recorded the rhyme "A Soule-cake, a Soule-cake, Have mercy on all Christen soules for a Soule-cake," and the Shropshire table set with "a high heap of soul-cakes, lying one upon another like the picture of the shewbread in the old Bibles," one for every visitor. Souling survived into the twentieth century in Shropshire, Cheshire, Lancashire and Staffordshire; the Antrobus souling play in Cheshire still has devils and a hobby horse with a real skull.

## Burns and the night of divination
The first dense, dated description of Halloween as a night of games is Robert Burns's poem "Halloween", written in 1785 and printed at Kilmarnock in 1786: twenty-eight stanzas of Ayrshire rites with the poet's own footnotes. Young women pulled kale stalks blindfold to read a future husband's shape, burned paired hazelnuts on the hearth to see whether two names burned quietly together or jumped apart, threw an apple peel over the shoulder to read an initial, and ducked for apples in a tub. The Emer glossary already ties Samain to divination, so the fortune-telling has a genuine medieval root. Apples and hazelnuts were the Otherworld's fruit in Irish story, which is why they carry the luck.

## Guising, turnips and Stingy Jack
Guising, going house to house disguised and earning food with a song or a trick, is documented in Scotland and Ireland; the first detailed printed account of Scottish guising is in Frank Leslie's Popular Monthly for November 1895, though the practice is older. The lantern was a job before it was a vegetable: "jack-o'-lantern" meant a night watchman, and before that the will-o'-the-wisp, the marsh light that leads travellers off the path. The Oxford English Dictionary dates the hollowed-vegetable sense to 1837. In Ireland and Scotland the vegetable was a turnip or a mangel-wurzel, scooped out, cut with a face, set on a windowsill or carried down a dark lane with a glowing coal inside; the Scots called them neep lanterns.

Folklore holds the lantern is Stingy Jack's: the story goes that a drunken Irish blacksmith tricked the Devil into becoming a coin and pocketed him, freed him on a promise never to take his soul, and when he died was refused by heaven and barred from hell, so the Devil threw him an ember and he has wandered since with it inside a turnip. That is legend, first set down in print in the nineteenth century, long after the Irish were carving turnips.

## How it became American Halloween
The Daily News of Kingston, Ontario, mentions pumpkin lanterns on 1 November 1866: the Irish and Scots found a bigger, softer vegetable in American fields and switched. Ruth Edna Kelley, a librarian in Lynn, Massachusetts, published the first book-length history, The Book of Hallowe'en, in 1919; she describes Scotch parties run from Burns's poem and going "a-souling as the English used," and never mentions anything resembling trick-or-treating. The phrase is Canadian: the Sault Daily Star of Ontario on 1 November 1917, then the Lethbridge Herald of 4 November 1927 reporting from Blackie, Alberta, that "the youthful tormentors were at back door and front demanding edible plunder by the word 'trick or treat'." The Bay City Daily Times printed it in Michigan on 1 November 1928; it was not near-universal in the United States until sugar rationing ended in 1947. The first Peanuts strip to use it ran in 1951.

## What the dead did that night, as people told it
In Irish and Scottish household custom collected in the nineteenth century, the family's dead came home on Hallowe'en. A place was laid for them, the fire kept in, food or drink left out, the door unbarred. Folklore holds that faces were blackened with bonfire ash or masked so the walking dead and the fairy host would take the living for their own kind and pass them by. Hutton's judgment stands beside all of it: the medieval texts give the loosened mounds and the week of feasting; the dead at the table are a later layer, real as custom, not provable as ancient religion.
