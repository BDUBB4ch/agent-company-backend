# Samhain and the Origins of Halloween

## What the oldest Irish texts actually say
The word is Samain (modern Samhain, said roughly SOW-in). A glossary attached to the tale Tochmarc Emire, "The Wooing of Emer", explains it: "Samuin, that is, summer's end." That tale, in its longer Middle Irish version of the eleventh or twelfth century, puts the words in Emer's mouth as a riddle and lists Samain first among the four quarter days with Imbolc, Beltaine and Lughnasa. It was the term-day of a cattle-keeping people: the day the herds came down from summer pasture, rents fell due and the slaughtering for winter began.

Serglige Con Culainn, "Cu Chulainn's Sickbed", says the Ulaid held their Samain assembly at Mag Muirthemne for a full week (three days either side and the day itself): feasting, drinking, trading, judging cases, contests. Echtra Nerai, "The Adventure of Nera", opens with Ailill and Medb at Rath Cruachan on Halloween and the line that the fairy-mounds of Ireland are always open about this time. On Ailill's dare, for a gold-hilted sword, Nera goes out in the dark to tie a withe round the foot of a hanged captive; the dead man speaks, asks to be carried on Nera's neck to a drink, and spits his last mouthful into the faces of a sleeping household, killing them; Nera then follows a host into the mound at Cruachan and comes back carrying summer flowers to prove where he has been. In the Fenian stories a being called Aillen comes to Tara every Samain, lulls the court asleep with music and burns the hall with fire from his mouth; the tale says he had done it for twenty-three years before the boy Fionn stayed awake by pressing a poisoned spear-point to his forehead and killed him. Lebor Gabala, the book of invasions, says the Fomorians took two-thirds of the corn, milk and children of Nemed's people every Samain: legend written down centuries later, but the one old passage tying the date to a tribute in children.

What the texts share: at Samain the boundary with the sid, the mounds of the Otherworld, is loosened, and things come out. The Irish Otherworld in these stories is not a land of the dead; it is a bright parallel country of the aos si, the people of the mounds, later softened in English to "fairies". The historian Ronald Hutton, who went through every medieval reference for The Stations of the Sun (1996) and again in Folklore (2024), concludes there is no record of a religious ceremony at Samain, no proof it was a festival of the dead, and none that it opened the year: a time when supernatural forces were guarded against or propitiated, and the dead came later, carried in by the Christian feast.

## Where the "Celtic New Year" and the "god Samhain" came from
In 1786 a British military engineer, Charles Vallancey, published a Vindication of the Ancient History of Ireland in which the Irish worshipped a judge of the dead called Saman; he rejected "summer's end" as a false derivation and read the name as "Bal-sab", lord of death. No Irish text, annal or manuscript names any such god. The "Celtic New Year" was argued by Sir John Rhys in the 1880s from a nineteenth-century Manx land-tenancy custom and made famous by James Frazer in The Golden Bough; Frazer hedged every sentence with "seems" and "may have," and the retellings dropped the hedges. Keating's history of Ireland, finished in 1634, is the sole source for the druids' great fire at Tlachtga (the Hill of Ward near Athboy, County Meath) on Samain eve, from which every hearth was relit; it rests on no earlier text. A 2014 dig on the hill found layers of intense burning; the archaeologist Steve Davis said "whether you want to equate that with Samhain or not" was another matter. The medieval druidic fire rite in the glossaries belongs to Beltaine, the first of May.

## The Christian date
The earliest solid evidence for All Saints on 1 November is a letter of Alcuin to Arno, bishop of Salzburg, around the year 800: "Kalendis Novembris solemnitas omnium sanctorum." The decree of 835, by which Louis the Pious with Pope Gregory IV ordered the feast kept "throughout Gaul and Germany", is known only from Sigebert of Gembloux in the twelfth century. All Souls, 2 November, the day for praying the dead out of purgatory, was fixed by Odilo, abbot of Cluny, for every house under the Cluniac rule, in 998 by the usual reckoning, after 1030 by the Catholic Encyclopedia's. Peter Damian's Life of Odilo says a pilgrim, storm-driven onto a rocky island, was shown by a hermit a chasm from which the groans of souls in torment rose, and carried the news to Cluny; Rome kept the day by the fourteenth century. No surviving document says the Church chose the date to swallow Samain; the two simply sit on the same three days, and Irish and Scottish custom ran them together as Hallowmas. The Scots shortening of All Hallows Even is first in print in the 1550s. Sixteenth-century English parish accounts pay for ringing the "soul bell" through Hallowmas night for the souls in purgatory; Elizabethan bishops spent decades trying to stop it.

## Souling and the soul cake
Praying for the dead was paid in bread. John Mirk's fourteenth-century Festial says "good men and women would this day buy bread and deal it for the souls that they loved, hoping with each loaf to get a soul out of purgatory." Shakespeare has Speed accuse his master of "puling, like a beggar at Hallowmas" (Two Gentlemen of Verona, II.i, 1593). John Aubrey, writing around 1686 to 1697, recorded the rhyme "A Soule-cake, a Soule-cake, Have mercy on all Christen soules for a Soule-cake," and the Shropshire table set with "a high heap of soul-cakes, lying one upon another like the picture of the shewbread in the old Bibles," one for every visitor. A correspondent to Notes and Queries on 15 November 1851 took down the Shropshire children's verses from the children themselves: "Soul! soul! for a soul-cake", then "one for Peter, two for Paul, three for Him who made us all." The cakes were small and round, spiced with saffron, studded with currants and scored with a deep cross, taken with spiced ale. Souling survived into the twentieth century in Shropshire, Cheshire, Lancashire and Staffordshire; the Antrobus souling play in Cheshire, revived in the late 1920s by Major A. W. Boyd, still has King George killing the Black Prince, a doctor raising him, a Beelzebub, and a Wild Horse that is a real horse's skull painted black on a pole with a man under the canvas.

## Wales: Nos Galan Gaeaf and the tailless black sow
The Welsh called it Nos Galan Gaeaf, the eve of the first day of winter, a spirit night when people kept away from churchyards, crossroads and stiles. Each district built a coelcerth, a hillside bonfire; apples and potatoes roasted in it while people danced and leapt and whooped round it, and before going home every person threw in a marked stone. Next morning they searched the ashes: a stone found clean meant luck, a stone missing meant its owner would die within the year. As the fire sank the shout went up, "Adref, adref, am y cyntaf, Hwch Ddu Gwta a gipio'r ola" (home, home, let each be first, the tailless black sow take the hindmost), and everyone ran, because the Hwch Ddu Gwta, the black sow with no tail, would take the last one on the hill. An Anglesey account written in 1888 says the shout had been common sixty years earlier. Supper was stwmp naw rhyw, the mash of nine sorts, with a ring in it for the next to marry.

## Burns and the night of divination, stanza by stanza
The first dense, dated description of Halloween as a night of games is Robert Burns's poem "Halloween", written in 1785 and printed at Kilmarnock in 1786: twenty-eight stanzas of Ayrshire rites with sixteen long footnotes in the poet's own voice, more than he gave any other poem. The company is "some merry, friendly, countra folks" met "to burn their nits, an' pou their stocks". First the kale: out "hand in hand, with eyes shut", pull the first stalk you meet, and its shape and the earth on its root foretell the husband's build and fortune, the taste of the pith his temper. Then the nuts, named for a lad and a lass and laid on the hearth: if they burn quietly side by side the match will hold, if one jumps and cracks away it will not. Merran steals out alone to the kiln, throws a blue clew of yarn into the pot and winds it back until something takes hold of the thread, and asks "wha hauds?" Wee Jenny wants to eat an apple before the glass with a candle, where the future husband's face looks over her shoulder. Jamie Fleck sows hemp-seed in the dark, dragging a harrow and chanting "hemp-seed, I saw thee", so that the one who will pull it follows at his back. Meg opens both barn doors and winnows with the wecht three times so the apparition will pass in at the windy door and out the other. Leezie goes to the burn where three lairds' lands meet to dip her left shirt-sleeve (hung before the fire, it is turned in the night by the apparition), and falls in the pool. Then the three luggies, one clean water, one foul, one empty: the blindfold hand that lands in the clean dish gets a maid, the foul a widow, the empty none, and Uncle John draws the empty dish three times running. The Emer glossary already ties Samain to divination, so the fortune-telling has a genuine medieval root. Apples and hazelnuts were the Otherworld's fruit in Irish story, which is why they carry the luck.

## Scotland's fires and guisers
The Callander entry in the Old Statistical Account, dated 31 October 1795, describes fires lit in every hamlet at sundown and the ashes raked into a circle with a stone near the rim for every person in the household; if any stone was moved or broken by morning the person it stood for was "fey", devoted to die within the twelvemonth. Fortingall in Perthshire lit its samhnag on the mound called Carn na Marbh, the cairn of the dead, every Halloween until 1924. In South Uist into the 1930s the guisers went out in sheepskin masks with painted faces and wigs of corn, carried lit peats from house to house, and gave a song or a joke for a bannock; the dish was fuarag, oatmeal and cream with a silver sixpence, a thimble and a button hidden in it. Guising is older than Halloween guising: Elgin's kirk session fined masked sword-dancers forty shillings each for guising at New Year 1623, when the dressing-up still belonged to the twelve days of Christmas. In the west the guisers opened with a play that began "Here I come, Galoshin", so the custom is still called galoshans there.

## Guising, turnips and Stingy Jack
Guising, going house to house disguised and earning food with a song or a trick, is documented in Scotland and Ireland; the first detailed printed account of Scottish guising is in Frank Leslie's Popular Monthly for November 1895, though the practice is older. The lantern was a job before it was a vegetable: the Oxford English Dictionary has "jack-o'-lantern" for the will-o'-the-wisp, the marsh light that leads travellers off the path, from 1658, for a night watchman from 1663 to 1704, and for the hollowed vegetable only from 1837. In Ireland and Scotland the vegetable was a turnip or a mangel-wurzel, scooped out, cut with a face, set on a windowsill or carried down a dark lane with a glowing coal inside; the Scots called them neep lanterns or tumshies.

Folklore holds the lantern is Stingy Jack's: the story goes that a drunken Irish blacksmith tricked the Devil into becoming a coin and pocketed him, freed him on a promise never to take his soul, and when he died was refused by heaven and barred from hell, so the Devil threw him an ember and he has wandered since with it inside a turnip. That is legend. It first appears in print in the Dublin Penny Journal of 16 January 1836 as "Jack o' the Lantern", a tale in which a nephew watching lights over a shaking bog calls them ignis fatuus, "gaseous vapour arising from putrid vegetable bodies", and his uncle credits them to "that misguided traveler, Jack o' the Lantern"; the 1836 telling never mentions a turnip or any vegetable at all. The Irish were carving turnips long before the story was written down.

## The night in Ireland, as people lived it
The Schools' Collection, written by about 50,000 schoolchildren in 1937 to 1939 from what their elders remembered, gives the texture. Colcannon, potatoes mashed with kale and a well of butter in the top, was the Halloween supper in almost every county; in Leinster and Connacht a spoonful was left out for the fairies, the Púca, or the poor souls. The barmbrack carried its ring, rag and thimble only at Halloween. Daniel Maclise went to a Halloween party at Blarney in October 1832 and painted it for the Royal Academy the next summer as Snap-Apple Night: a man lunges with his teeth at an apple stuck on a spinning wooden cross whose other arms hold lit candles, a courting couple burn nuts on the grate, and girls pour molten lead through the wards of a key into cold water to read the shapes; the night was also called Nut Crack Night. The fairies were out after twelve and would take you away with them. It was Púca's Night, when the Púca went through the country spitting on every berry left on the bushes, so that no haw, sloe or blackberry was eaten after it; a Longford child wrote that on Hallow Eve the Púca poisons the blackberries. Masks were made to frighten the neighbours, and bands of men and children, freed by the disguise, went visiting.

## How it became American Halloween
The Daily News of Kingston, Ontario, mentions pumpkin lanterns on 1 November 1866: the Irish and Scots found a bigger, softer vegetable in American fields and switched. Ruth Edna Kelley, a librarian in Lynn, Massachusetts, published the first book-length history, The Book of Hallowe'en, in 1919; she describes Scotch parties run from Burns's poem and going "a-souling as the English used," and never mentions anything resembling trick-or-treating. The phrase is Canadian: the Sault Daily Star of Ontario printed "tricks or treats" on 1 November 1917, then the Lethbridge Herald of 4 November 1927 reported from Blackie, Alberta, that "the youthful tormentors were at back door and front demanding edible plunder by the word 'trick or treat'." The Bay City Daily Times printed it in Michigan on 1 November 1928.

The pranks were the point. By the 1920s American Halloween was gates off hinges and soaped windows; by 1933 cities were discussing banning the night; the Milwaukee Sentinel called 1938 "one of the most riotous orgies of Halloween violence ever recorded here." Des Moines invented Beggars' Night in 1938, moving the door-to-door to 30 October with the greeting "soap or eats". In November 1939 Doris Hudson Moss wrote in The American Home, under the title "A Victim of the Window-Soaping Brigade?", that if you give the children food and a welcome "the rowdies from the other side of town will join the party spirit and leave your front gate intact". It was not near-universal in the United States until sugar rationing ended in 1947; Peanuts ran three Halloween strips on 29 to 31 October 1951, and Disney's cartoon Trick or Treat opened on 10 October 1952. The 1950s suburbs put young families on walkable streets of closely spaced houses; Ben Cooper of Brooklyn sold the costume as a vinyl smock and a plastic mask; the candy makers advertised wrapped sweets as the modern thing to hand out. In Philadelphia in 1950 Mary Emma and Clyde Allison's Sunday-school children collected coins door to door for UNICEF milk, "Trick or Treat for All the World's Children", and brought in under twenty dollars in nickels and dimes.

## The poisoned candy, and what the record shows
In 1964 Helen Pfeil of Greenlawn, Long Island, handed children she judged too old to be begging arsenic ant-poison buttons, dog biscuits and steel wool as a joke, and was arrested; no one was hurt. On Halloween night 1974, in Texas, eight-year-old Timothy O'Bryan died of cyanide in a giant Pixy Stix; his father Ronald Clark O'Bryan, who had just insured each of his children, had laced it, handed poisoned straws to his daughter and three other children to cover the crime (none ate theirs), and was executed in Texas on 31 March 1984. The sociologist Joel Best went through every Halloween report in the New York Times, the Los Angeles Times and the Chicago Tribune from 1958 to 1984 for a 1985 paper in Social Problems, "The Razor Blade in the Apple": fewer than ninety reports in all, most of them hoaxes or children's own tampering, and not one child killed or seriously hurt by a treat from a stranger. The 1982 Tylenol murders in Chicago, seven dead of cyanide-laced capsules a month before Halloween, led towns in Illinois, Ohio, Pennsylvania and Massachusetts to cancel trick-or-treating. The apple, once the standard treat, never came back.

## What the dead did that night, as people told it
In Irish and Scottish household custom collected in the nineteenth century, the family's dead came home on Hallowe'en. A place was laid for them, the fire built up and the hearth swept (Manorhamilton, Leitrim: "a blazing fire and a well swept hearth to welcome their unearthly visitors"), the lamp left lit, the door left unbolted, the table left set; in Meath no water was thrown out after dark for fear of splashing the Holy Souls; in Clare a girl who stood at midnight before a mirror with a lit candle would see the one she would marry. Folklore holds that faces were blackened with bonfire ash or masked so the walking dead and the fairy host would take the living for their own kind and pass them by. Hutton's judgment stands beside all of it: the medieval texts give the loosened mounds and the week of feasting; the dead at the table are a later layer, real as custom, not provable as ancient religion.
