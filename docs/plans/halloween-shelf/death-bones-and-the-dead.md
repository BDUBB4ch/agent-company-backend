# Death, Bones and the Dead

## What a body does after death
The heart stops and the cells begin digesting themselves within minutes. In the first hours the body cools (algor mortis), blood sinks to the lowest surfaces and stains them purple (livor mortis), and from about three hours the muscles lock (rigor mortis). That is the fresh stage, roughly two days.

Then the gut bacteria eat outward. The belly swells, sometimes to twice its size, the tongue and eyes protrude, the skin marbles green and black along the veins, and bloody froth, purge fluid, pushes out of the mouth and nose. Bloat runs from about day two to day six in temperate weather, longer in cold. In active decay, days five to eleven, the skin slips off in sheets, the tissues liquefy into the ground, maggots seethe in a mass warm to the touch, and the body loses most of its weight. By the third or fourth week there is leather, cartilage and bone.

The vampire signs are all on that list. A corpse dug up in winter after a few weeks is swollen with gas, so the villagers see a plump, well-fed body; the skin has slipped and dried back from the nails and hairline, so nails and hair look grown; the purge fluid at the mouth is the "fresh blood"; and when a stake goes into a gas-filled chest it groans and spurts.

## The Serbian vampire panics
In 1725 the Imperial Provisor Ernst Frombald, an Austrian official in newly annexed Serbia, watched the villagers of Kisilova dig up a peasant named Peter Plogojowitz ten weeks after burial. Nine people had died in eight days, each saying on the deathbed that Plogojowitz had come in the night and throttled them. Frombald reported that he smelled none of the usual odour of the dead; that apart from the nose, which had fallen in, the body was "completely fresh"; that the old skin and nails had peeled and new ones grown; and that when they drove a sharpened stake through the heart "completely fresh" blood ran from the ears and mouth. They burned the body.

Seven years later, in Medvegia, a dead soldier named Arnold Paole was blamed for a second wave: seventeen dead in under three months. On 7 January 1732 regimental surgeon Johann Flückinger opened thirteen graves with officers watching. Their report, Visum et Repertum, "Seen and Found," signed at Belgrade on 26 January, lists the bodies: a woman named Stana, dead twenty days, "quite complete and undecayed," her chest full of liquid blood; a man named Milloe, dead six weeks, whose blood ran from the nose "in abundance" when the head was cut off. They declared such bodies "in the vampire condition"; the heads were cut off, the bodies burned and the ashes thrown in the Morava. The report was printed in Nuremberg that year and made "vampire" an English word.

## Ossuaries and bone churches
When a churchyard filled, the old bones came up to make room. At Sedlec near Kutná Hora in Bohemia the plague of the fourteenth century put some 30,000 bodies in the cemetery and the wars that followed some 10,000 more. The bones were stacked in the church crypt, the remains of between 40,000 and 70,000 people, until 1870, when a woodcarver named František Rint was hired to bleach and arrange them: chains of skulls across the doorways, the Schwarzenberg coat of arms, and a chandelier said to contain at least one of every bone in the body.

In Paris the Cemetery of the Holy Innocents had taken the city's dead for six centuries. From December 1785 the bones were carted at night into the old limestone quarries under the Left Bank, consecrated as the Municipal Ossuary on 7 April 1786. Between six and seven million people lie there under the lintel that reads "Arrête! C'est ici l'empire de la Mort."

The Capuchin friars of Rome began carrying bones into the crypt of Santa Maria della Concezione on the Via Veneto in 1631; the bones of 3,700 friars fill five rooms. A placard in the Crypt of the Three Skeletons reads: "Quello che voi siete noi eravamo; quello che noi siamo voi sarete," what you are we once were, what we are you will be. In Palermo the Capuchins mummified Brother Silvestro of Gubbio in 1599 and went on for three centuries; about 8,000 bodies hang in their corridors in their own clothes. The last was Rosalia Lombardo, two years old, dead of pneumonia in 1920, embalmed by Alfredo Salafia so well that her eyelashes are still in place.

## Memento mori and the dance of death
Between August 1424 and Lent 1425 a mural was painted along the open arcade of the charnel house at the Holy Innocents: a procession in which a grinning corpse takes each man by the hand, pope, emperor, cardinal, king, down to the labourer and the child, every corpse mocking its partner; the fat abbot is told that the fattest rots first. The wall was demolished in 1669, but the printer Guyot Marchant had copied it in woodcuts on 28 September 1485 and the book sold across France. That is the Danse Macabre.

## Sin-eaters, wakes and the laying-out
John Aubrey wrote down the earliest account around 1686: an old custom at Herefordshire funerals of hiring a poor man, "a long, leane, ugly, lamentable poor raskal," to eat a loaf laid on the corpse's chest and drink a bowl of beer over it, for sixpence, so that he took upon himself "ipso facto" the sins of the dead and freed them from walking afterward. The last known sin-eater was Richard Munslow of Ratlinghope, Shropshire, a respected farmer who seems to have taken up the custom after three of his children died of scarlet fever in one week in 1870. He died on 23 April 1906.

In an Irish house of the nineteenth century the clocks were stopped at the moment of death, the mirrors turned to the wall so the soul would not be caught, the window nearest the bed opened for it to leave. The body was laid out in the main room and never left alone. The bean chaointe, the keening women, waited until the body was ready, then began: a lead voice chanting verses to the dead by name, the others swaying and wailing under it.

## Buried alive
In 1829 Dr Johann Gottfried Taberger patented a coffin with strings tied to the hands, head and feet and run up a tube to a bell above the grave; if the bell rang, the night watchman was to pump air down a second tube with a bellows until the grave could be opened. No safety coffin is recorded as ever having saved anyone.

## Burke and Hare
In Edinburgh in 1828 the anatomist Robert Knox paid seven to ten pounds for a fresh body. William Burke and William Hare sold him sixteen people in ten months, Hare pinning the victim and Burke kneeling on the chest with a hand over nose and mouth, which left no marks. Burke was hanged in the Lawnmarket on 28 January 1829 before a crowd put at 25,000, dissected in public by Alexander Monro, who dipped his pen in the blood and wrote "This is written with the blood of Wm Burke, who was hanged at Edinburgh"; a pocketbook bound in his tanned skin, inscribed "Executed 28 Jan 1829," sits in Surgeons' Hall.

## The Black Death, the beak and the lancet
Boccaccio, in Florence in 1348, wrote that the sickness showed first as a swelling in the groin or armpit, "some as large as an ordinary apple, some the size of an egg," which the people called gavoccioli, after which black and livid blotches spread over the arms and thighs, and most died within three days. Between a third and a half of Europe died in five years; the buboes, when they burst, ran with blood and pus. The beaked doctor belongs to a later plague: Charles de Lorme, physician to Louis XIII, described in 1619 a suit of Moroccan goat leather with a mask whose "nose half a foot long, shaped like a beak," was stuffed with dried flowers, herbs, camphor or a vinegar sponge against the smell believed to carry the disease. Through all of it the doctors bled their patients to balance Galen's four humours, blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. On 14 December 1799 George Washington, choking with a throat infection, was bled four times in a day, roughly three quarts, about half the blood in his body, and died that night.
