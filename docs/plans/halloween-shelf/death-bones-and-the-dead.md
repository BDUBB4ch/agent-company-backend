# Death, Bones and the Dead

## What a body does after death
The heart stops and the cells begin digesting themselves within minutes. In the first hours the body cools (algor mortis), blood sinks and stains the lowest surfaces purple (livor mortis), and from about three hours the muscles lock (rigor mortis) for a day and a half. That is the fresh stage, roughly two days; blowflies have found the body within hours, laid in the eyes, nose and mouth, and the eggs hatch in a day or two.

Then the gut bacteria eat outward. The belly swells, sometimes to twice its size, the tongue and eyes protrude, the skin marbles green and black along the veins, and bloody froth, purge fluid, pushes out of the mouth and nose; the smell is two molecules named for what they are, putrescine and cadaverine. Bloat runs from about day two to day six in temperate weather, longer in cold. In active decay, days five to eleven, the skin slips off in sheets, the tissues liquefy into the ground in a dark island that kills the grass, maggots seethe in a mass warm to the touch, and the body loses most of its weight. By the third or fourth week there is leather, cartilage and bone. In cold wet ground without air the fat turns instead to grave wax, adipocere, which can hold a face for a century. It is known by the day because William Bass opened the Body Farm at Knoxville, Tennessee, in 1981 and let donated bodies rot in the open under observation.

The vampire signs are all on that list. A corpse dug up in winter after a few weeks is swollen with gas, so the villagers see a plump, well-fed body; the skin has slipped and dried back from the nails and hairline, so nails and hair look grown; the purge fluid at the mouth is the "fresh blood"; and when a stake goes into a gas-filled chest it groans and spurts.

## The Serbian vampire panics
In 1725 the Imperial Provisor Ernst Frombald, an Austrian official in newly annexed Serbia, watched the villagers of Kisilova dig up a peasant named Peter Plogojowitz ten weeks after burial. Nine people had died in eight days, each saying on the deathbed that Plogojowitz had come in the night and throttled them. Frombald reported that he smelled none of the usual odour of the dead; that apart from the nose, which had fallen in, the body was "completely fresh"; that the old skin and nails had peeled and new ones grown; and that when they drove a sharpened stake through the heart "completely fresh" blood ran from the ears and mouth. They burned the body.

Seven years later, in Medvegia, a dead soldier named Arnold Paole was blamed for a second wave: seventeen dead in under three months. On 7 January 1732 regimental surgeon Johann Flückinger opened thirteen graves with officers watching. Their report, Visum et Repertum, "Seen and Found," signed at Belgrade on 26 January, lists the bodies: a woman named Stana, dead twenty days, "quite complete and undecayed," her chest full of liquid blood; a man named Milloe, dead six weeks, whose blood ran from the nose "in abundance" when the head was cut off. They declared such bodies "in the vampire condition"; the heads were cut off, the bodies burned and the ashes thrown in the Morava. The report was printed in Nuremberg that year and made "vampire" an English word. In 1755 Maria Theresa sent her physician Gerard van Swieten to Moravia, where villagers were still burning their dead; he found a natural cause for every sign, and she forbade the opening of graves.

## Ossuaries and bone churches
When a churchyard filled, the old bones came up to make room. At Sedlec near Kutná Hora in Bohemia the story goes that an abbot brought a handful of earth from Golgotha home in 1278 and scattered it on the cemetery, after which half of central Europe wanted to be buried there. The plague of the fourteenth century put some 30,000 bodies in the ground and the Hussite wars some 10,000 more, and legend has it that a half-blind monk stacked the bones into pyramids in 1511. In 1870 the Schwarzenberg family hired a woodcarver named František Rint to put the crypt in order. He bleached the bones and built from them: garlands of skulls across the vaults, chalices, monstrances, pyramids of skulls in the side chapels, a chandelier said to contain at least one of every bone in the body, and the Schwarzenberg coat of arms, in whose lower right quarter a raven of bone pecks the eye out of a Turk's head, a real skull, the family's badge since Adolf von Schwarzenberg beat the Ottomans in 1598. Rint signed the wall by the door in hand and arm bones. Between 40,000 and 70,000 people are in that one room.

In Paris the Cemetery of the Holy Innocents had taken the city's dead for six centuries, in pits that held hundreds at a time. In 1780, after weeks of rain, the wall between the cemetery and a neighbouring house gave way and a burial pit emptied into the cellar. From December 1785 the bones were carted at night, the carts draped in black, priests walking beside them chanting the office of the dead by torchlight, into the old limestone quarries under the Left Bank, consecrated as the Municipal Ossuary on 7 April 1786. At first the bones were tipped down a shaft; from 1810 the quarry inspector Héricart de Thury had them stacked into the walls you see now, long bones packed like firewood with rows of skulls set in them in bands and crosses. The lintel reads "Arrête! C'est ici l'empire de la Mort," stop, this is the empire of Death. Between six and seven million people lie there.

The Capuchin friars of Rome moved to Santa Maria della Concezione on the Via Veneto in 1631 and brought the bones of their dead with them in 300 cartloads. Five low rooms of bone open off a corridor: the Crypt of the Skulls, the Crypt of the Pelvises, where hip bones are set in the walls in patterns like moths, the Crypt of the Leg Bones and Thigh Bones, the Crypt of the Resurrection, and the Crypt of the Three Skeletons, where a small skeleton inside an oval of bone holds a scythe in one hand and scales in the other. The bones of about 3,700 friars, buried between 1528 and 1870, fill the rooms; each lay in the soil about thirty years before his bones went into the walls and a brother took his place. The placard in the last room reads: "Quello che voi siete noi eravamo; quello che noi siamo voi sarete," what you are we once were, what we are you will be.

In Palermo the Capuchins put Brother Silvestro of Gubbio in their crypt in 1599 and went on for three centuries. A body was laid on a rack of ceramic pipes in a sealed drying room, the colatoio, for about eight months until it had drained, then washed with vinegar, stuffed with straw, dressed in its own clothes and hung on a hook or laid in a niche in the corridor for its kind: friars, priests, professionals, men, women, virgins, children. About 8,000 people are there, some still with faces, most of them bone in clothes. The last was Rosalia Lombardo, not yet two, dead of pneumonia on 6 December 1920, embalmed by Alfredo Salafia so well that her eyelashes are still in place. His formula was lost until 2009, when the curator Dario Piombino-Mascali found it in Salafia's papers: formalin to kill the bacteria, zinc salts to stiffen the body, alcohol with salicylic acid against fungus, glycerin to keep the skin from drying. The stories that she opens her eyes come from the light: the lids were never quite closed, and as the sun moves across the window the slit of blue beneath shows and hides.

At Czermna in Polish Silesia the parish priest Wacław Tomaszek and a gravedigger spent the years from 1776 digging up the mass graves of the Thirty Years' War, cholera and plague and lining the walls and ceiling of a small chapel with about 3,000 skulls and long bones; another 20,000 to 30,000 are under the floor. On the altar are the skulls of Tomaszek himself, placed there after his death in 1804, the gravedigger, the village headman and his wife, a Tatar, and the femur of a man two metres tall.

At Hallstatt in Austria the churchyard is a shelf between mountain and lake, so graves were reopened after some years and the bones moved to the charnel house. From about 1720 the families painted the skulls: bleached for weeks in the sun, then the name and dates lettered on the forehead with a cross and a wreath round it, roses for love, ivy for life, oak for glory, laurel for victory. About 1,200 skulls are stacked there, some 610 painted. The last was added in 1995 at her own request: a woman who died in 1983, with a gold tooth.

## Memento mori and the dance of death
Between August 1424 and Lent 1425 a mural was painted along the open arcade of the charnel house at the Holy Innocents: a procession in which a grinning corpse takes each man by the hand, pope, emperor, cardinal, king, down to the labourer and the child, every corpse mocking its partner in verse; the fat abbot is told that the fattest rots first. The wall was demolished in 1669, but the printer Guyot Marchant had copied it in woodcuts with its verses on 28 September 1485 and the book sold across France. That is the Danse Macabre. Bernt Notke painted a thirty-metre dance for the Marienkirche in Lübeck in 1463; a copy replaced it in 1701, and that burned on the night of Palm Sunday 1942 when the city was bombed. Berlin's, twenty-two metres in the porch of its Marienkirche, was whitewashed at the Reformation and found under the plaster in 1860. Hans Holbein the Younger drew it small, forty-one woodcuts printed in Lyon in 1538: Death drives the ploughman's horses, drags the abbot off by his habit, pulls the duchess out of bed, leads the small child away by the hand. Older than all of it is the Three Living and the Three Dead, written down about 1280: three young lords out hunting meet three corpses, who tell them, "As you are, so I was; as I am, so you will be," the line that ended up on gravestones for six hundred years.

## Sin-eaters, wakes and the laying-out
John Aubrey wrote down the earliest account around 1686: an old custom at Herefordshire funerals of hiring a poor man, "a long, leane, ugly, lamentable poor raskal" who lived in a cottage on the Ross highway, to eat a loaf passed across the corpse and drink a mazard bowl of beer over it, for sixpence, so that he took upon himself "ipso facto" the sins of the dead and freed them from walking afterward. The last known sin-eater was Richard Munslow of Ratlinghope, Shropshire, a respected farmer of seventy acres who seems to have taken up the custom after three of his children died of scarlet fever in one week in 1870. He died on 23 April 1906; the parish restored his grave in 2010.

In an Irish house of the nineteenth century the clocks were stopped at the moment of death, the mirrors turned to the wall so the soul would not be caught, the window nearest the bed opened for it to leave. The body was laid out in the main room and never left alone; clay pipes, tobacco and snuff went round, and whiskey. The bean chaointe, the keening women, waited until the body was ready, then began: a lead voice chanting verses to the dead by name, the others swaying and wailing under it. Late at night came the wake games, feats of strength, mock trials, pranks on the sleepers, which the bishops condemned and could not stop. In England coins were laid on the eyelids to hold them shut and a plate of coarse salt set on the chest to keep the body from swelling; the plates turn up in Georgian and Victorian graves at Hull. The coffin went out feet first so the dead could not look back and call another after them. Across England, Wales, Germany and New England the bees were told: someone knocked three times on the hive, said who had died, tied black crape to it and left a piece of the funeral biscuit, or the bees would die or leave. On 9 September 2022 the Buckingham Palace beekeeper John Chapple knocked on each hive, tied a black ribbon to it and told the bees the Queen was dead and that "your new master will be a good master to you."

## Buried alive
Duke Ferdinand of Brunswick ordered the first recorded safety coffin before he died in 1792: a window, an air tube, and a lock in place of nails with the keys in his shroud. In 1829 Dr Johann Gottfried Taberger patented a coffin with strings tied to the hands, head and feet and run up a tube to a bell above the grave; if the bell rang, the night watchman was to pump air down a second tube with a bellows until the grave could be opened. The grandest was Count Karnice-Karnicki's of 1897, chamberlain to the Tsar: a spring-loaded ball on the chest which, at the least breath, opened an air pipe, lit a lamp, raised a flag and rang a bell; shown at the Sorbonne, it failed when an assistant was buried to demonstrate it and he had to be dug out. Germany tried the other way: from 1791 Christoph Wilhelm Hufeland's Leichenhaus at Weimar kept eight bodies in a steam-warmed hall with a string from every finger to a bell and a watchman who was never to leave, and some fifty such waiting mortuaries were built up to 1860, the bodies kept until they visibly rotted. No safety coffin or waiting mortuary is recorded as ever having saved anyone.

## The resurrection men
Surgeons could teach only on the dead, and the dead came by night. The tools were a wooden spade, quieter than iron, a dark lantern, iron hooks and a rope: dig down at the head end of a fresh grave, break the top of the coffin lid with the hooks, rope under the arms, haul the body out through the hole and off in a sack. About 1800 a body fetched two guineas in London; by 1828, when the surgeon Astley Cooper testified to Parliament, it was eight and sometimes fourteen. One London gang member, probably Joseph Naples, kept a diary from November 1811 to December 1812, now at the Royal College of Surgeons, listing night by night the graveyards worked, the bodies taken and the money, with nothing for May, June and July because the schools were closed. The living answered with iron: a coffin collar bolted round the neck to the floor of the coffin, and the mortsafe, an iron cage locked over the grave and hired from the parish for about six weeks, the time it took for a body to be of no use to a surgeon; two still stand in Greyfriars kirkyard in Edinburgh. In Ohio Philip Clover patented a coffin torpedo in 1878, an iron pipe packed with gunpowder and lead balls, its trigger wires tied to the arms and ankles of the corpse.

## Burke and Hare
In Edinburgh in 1828 the anatomist Robert Knox paid seven to ten pounds for a fresh body. It began in November 1827 when a pensioner called Old Donald died in William Hare's lodging house owing four pounds in rent, and Hare and his lodger William Burke carried the body to Knox for seven pounds ten shillings. After that they stopped waiting. Mary Paterson, eighteen, in April; the story goes that Knox kept her three months in whisky and had artists draw her. Daft Jamie, a lame beggar known to all Edinburgh, in October, who fought them both; students recognised him on the table. Sixteen people in ten months, Hare pinning the victim and Burke kneeling on the chest with a hand over nose and mouth, which left no marks and gave the language the word burking. The last was Margaret Docherty, brought home on Halloween, 31 October 1828; the lodgers James and Ann Gray found her under the straw of the bed next morning and went to the police. The trial opened on Christmas Eve and ran twenty-four hours without a break; on Christmas morning Burke was guilty, Helen McDougal not proven, and he said to her, "Nelly, you're out of the scrape." Hare had turned king's evidence. Burke was hanged in the Lawnmarket on 28 January 1829 before a crowd put at 25,000, and dissected in public by Alexander Monro, who dipped his pen in the blood and wrote "This is written with the blood of Wm Burke, who was hanged at Edinburgh"; two thousand students nearly rioted to get in and thirty thousand of the public filed past the body next day. His skeleton is in the university's anatomical museum and a pocketbook bound in his tanned skin, inscribed "Executed 28 Jan 1829," sits in Surgeons' Hall. Hare was let out on 5 February and was never seen again. In London John Bishop and Thomas Williams drugged boys with rum and laudanum and lowered them head first into their garden well in Bethnal Green; when they offered an Italian street boy, Carlo Ferrari, to King's College in November 1831 the staff saw a body too fresh for the story. They were hanged at Newgate on 5 December 1831, and the next summer the Anatomy Act gave the schools the unclaimed dead of the workhouses instead.

## The Black Death, the beak and the lancet
Genoese galleys brought it into Messina in October 1347. Boccaccio, in Florence in 1348, wrote that the sickness showed first as a swelling in the groin or armpit, "some as large as an ordinary apple, some the size of an egg," which the people called gavoccioli, after which black and livid blotches spread over the arms and thighs, and most died within three days; he reckoned more than a hundred thousand dead inside the walls between March and July. Agnolo di Tura of Siena wrote: "And I, Agnolo di Tura, called the Fat, buried my five children with my own hands." The Florentine chronicler Marchionne di Coppo Stefani said the pits were dug at every church down to the water table, bodies laid in and earth shovelled thin over them, then more bodies and more earth, "as one makes lasagne with layers of pasta and cheese." Between a third and a half of Europe died in five years; the buboes, when they burst, ran with blood and pus.

London's last great plague was 1665. Samuel Pepys saw his first red crosses on 7 June, on doors in Drury Lane with "Lord have mercy upon us" beneath, "the first of that kind that to my remembrance I ever saw." The dead were counted by the searchers, old parish women paid to look at every corpse and name the cause, who could be bribed to write "fever" so the house would not be shut up with a watchman at the door. In the week of 19 to 26 September the bill listed 7,165 dead of plague in 126 parishes. On 16 October Pepys wrote, "But Lord, how empty the streets are, and melancholy, so many poor sick people in the streets, full of sores." The bills record 68,596 plague deaths; the real figure is put near 100,000, a quarter of the city. Defoe's Journal of the Plague Year, with its dead-cart, its bellman and its pit in Aldgate churchyard holding 1,114 bodies, is a novel of 1722 built on that year's records.

The beaked doctor belongs to the later plagues, not the Black Death. Charles de Lorme, physician to Louis XIII, described in 1619 a suit of Moroccan goat leather with a mask whose "nose half a foot long, shaped like a beak," was stuffed with dried flowers, herbs, camphor or a vinegar sponge against the smell believed to carry the disease. The picture everyone knows, "Der Doctor Schnabel von Rom," is a satirical print sold by Paulus Fürst in Nuremberg in 1656, the year plague killed about 300,000 in Naples.

Through all of it the doctors bled their patients to balance Galen's four humours, blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile, which made a man sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric or melancholy. King Charles II fell down in a fit on 2 February 1685; his physicians took sixteen ounces from his arm and eight by cupping, blistered his scalp, put plasters of pigeon dung on his feet, bled him from the jugular, gave him forty drops of spirit of human skull, and he died on 6 February. On 14 December 1799 George Washington, choking with a throat infection, was bled four times in a day, roughly three quarts, about half the blood in his body, and died that night. Lord Byron, feverish at Missolonghi in April 1824, was bled and leeched of near two pounds of blood and died on 19 April. France imported about 41 million leeches in 1833 alone.

## The Victorian way of death
In the 1840s a daguerreotype cost a working family about a week's wages, far below a painted portrait, so for many children the only picture ever made of them was made after they died: laid as if asleep, or in the mother's lap with her face hidden under a drape, the "hidden mother" of so many plates, who held a living child still through a long exposure as often as a dead one. The iron posing stand behind a figure's feet is not a sign of a corpse; the living needed it. After Prince Albert died in 1861 Queen Victoria wore black for the rest of her life, and a widow spent a year in dull black covered in crape, a stiff crimped silk gauze, then six months with less crape, six in plain black, and six of half mourning in grey and mauve, with jet jewellery only toward the end. Jay's, the London General Mourning Warehouse on Regent Street, founded 1841, sold the whole kit from the dress to the coffin, the hearse and the black horses.
