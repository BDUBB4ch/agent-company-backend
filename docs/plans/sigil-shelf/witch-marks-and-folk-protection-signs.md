# Witch Marks and Folk Protection Signs

A study shelf, not a pattern book: what is recorded about marks made to keep harm out of houses, barns, churches and bodies. Drawings are SVG path strings in a 0-100 box, one string per stroke, in drawing order, labelled "faithful" (matches the recorded geometry) or "approximate" (recorded examples vary; this is one honest instance). What cannot be drawn faithfully is described in words. Legend is marked as legend.

## 1. Witch marks in English and European buildings

Historic England calls them ritual protection or apotropaic ("turning away" evil) marks, usually carved near entrances to protect the people inside from witches and evil spirits. The National Trust finds them in barns, houses, churches, on furniture and in caves, usually near windows, doorways and fireplaces, mostly in buildings from the medieval period to the early 1800s; the evil feared may have been demons or the evil eye as much as witches. Timothy Easton found daisy wheels commonest on stable doors and farm buildings. The Norfolk Medieval Graffiti Survey (Matthew Champion, from 2010) had by 2015 recorded over 28,000 inscriptions in about half of Norfolk's 650 or so medieval churches; compass-drawn flower designs are its commonest motif and cluster around fonts, which Champion reads as protection for the baptised. Concealed shoes, horse skulls and witch bottles were hidden at the same openings.

Caution: in July 2026 Jennifer Alexander (Warwick University) said there is "no evidence" these are witches' marks: daisy wheels are masons' compass practice and W and M shapes masons' ciphers, though some symbols may have been against the evil eye. Some marks are surely workmen's; clustering at openings and fonts argues others were made to protect.

### Daisy wheel (hexafoil)

The commonest mark: a compass-drawn circle with six petals inside, single or in interlinked groups. At Tudor House, Worcester, one 4 cm across on a fireplace stone was drawn with compasses, then cut with hammer and chisel. Historic England calls it a single endless line that supposedly confused and trapped evil spirits; the belief was that demons following a line became trapped. Hoggard reads circles and daisy wheels as sun signs bringing light into darkness, and records that witches hate circles, having no corners to hide in.

### Marian marks: VV, M, AM, PM

Letters calling on the Virgin Mary to protect the place. Overlapping V's are read as Virgo Virginum ("Virgin of Virgins") and can form a W; a lone M as Maria; V and M also appear intertwined. Historic England reads AM as Ave Maria and PM as Pace Maria. Hoggard's test: overlapping V's or an isolated M are the Virgin's initials; letters among other letters are more likely someone's initials or a Latin phrase. These are readings, not self-explaining inscriptions.

Drawing, faithful to the common form (two overlapping V's; proportions vary):

- "M 15 20 L 35 80 L 55 20"
- "M 45 20 L 65 80 L 85 20"

### Mesh and ladder marks

Historic England describes the mesh as a crosshatch thought to work like a net for demonic forces. Hoggard calls it "either a grid or a tangle of marks designed to form a barrier"; one Shropshire house has one on the back of every shutter. A grid is sometimes called a Jacob's ladder. On a bedroom ceiling at Woolpit, Suffolk, Easton found symbols drawn in tallow-candle smoke ("candle writing"), probably later 17th century, including grid-iron and ladder shapes; he linked them to Twelfth Night "rough music", household objects banged to drive spirits out. Others read such grids as spirit traps; experts disagree.

### Rings, crosses, pentangles

The John Moore Museum, Tewkesbury, describes a "bull's eye" of three concentric circles against the evil eye. The National Trust also lists crosses, mazes and diagonal lines; crisscrossing lines are said to confuse a following spirit. Champion calls the pentangle a Christian protection against demons, though uncommon in churches. A plain X (saltire) appears as protection only in modern advice, so it is not given.

Concentric rings (bull's eye), faithful to the three-circle description; spacing varies:

- "M 50 24 A 26 26 0 0 1 50 76 A 26 26 0 0 1 50 24"
- "M 50 38 A 12 12 0 0 1 50 62 A 12 12 0 0 1 50 38"

### Burn marks

Teardrop scorches, often on a hearth lintel but on timbers anywhere: over 80 in the Tower of London, a row of ten at the King's House, Lyndhurst. Dean and Hill (Vernacular Architecture, 2014) tested flames on green oak: a deep mark needed the carbon scraped away at intervals. In 2021 James Wright found rushlights burned randomly, while a wax taper held at 45 degrees to the timber made the teardrop. Easton suggests inoculation by sympathetic magic: a controlled flame guards against uncontrolled fire. A burn mark is made by flame and time, not a path.

### Knole House, Kent

In November 2014 MOLA (Museum of London Archaeology) reported marks in the Upper King's Room, built for James I after the Gunpowder Plot of 1605. The oak was felled in winter 1605-06 and used green, in place by summer 1606. On beams and joists under the floor and on the fireplace surround were interlocking V's and checkerboard "demon traps", beside ordinary carpenters' marks; James Wright also recorded taper scorch marks on a floor joist. The work was done by 1608; the owner died that year and the king never came. The Plot link is a reading: Francis Young wrote "we really have very little idea what apotropaic marks were actually for", and workmen may have cut them whenever they laid a floor.

### Creswell Crags, Nottinghamshire and Derbyshire

Hayley Clark and Ed Waters of Subterranea Britannica noticed marks, long dismissed as graffiti, on a cave tour (date not given). On 15 February 2019 the site announced perhaps Britain's largest collection: "high hundreds in one cave alone" (the previous cave record was 57, in Somerset), later found in every cave, on walls and ceilings, often over dark holes and crevices. Forms: VV, PM, and diagonal lines, boxes and mazes thought to trap evil, added over time; Ronald Hutton calls them medieval and early modern.

### Historic England's survey

On Halloween 2016 Historic England asked the public for photographs and locations of marks, never fully recorded. A reported total of over 600 responses is unconfirmed.

## 2. Pennsylvania Dutch hex signs and braucherei

Braucherei, or powwow (an Algonquian loanword meaning a healer), is a Christian folk practice of the Pennsylvania Dutch, formed in the 1700s from what German-speaking migrants brought: incantations, often under the breath, Bible verses and saints' names, to treat people, livestock and crops and to give protection. Brauchers were called on to lift a Hexe's (witch's) curse. John George Hohman, in Pennsylvania from 1802, published "Der lange verborgene Freund" in 1820, in English "The Long Lost Friend". Its charms include "Trotter Head, I forbid thee my house and premises", written and placed in the stable against bewitched cattle and on the bedstead against spirits that "nightly torment old and young people"; I. N. I. R. I. with SANCTUS SPIRITUS on white paper, carried "against evil spirits and all manner of witchcraft"; and the Sator square, fed to cattle against witchcraft or thrown into a fire on a plate to put it out.

Barn stars are a separate tradition that later took on that reputation. Star designs on barns date to at least the early 1800s, large ones from about 1830, spreading with ready-mixed paint and peaking in the early 20th century; six-, eight- and twelve-pointed. Patrick Donmoyer (Kutztown University) says they were part of farm life, not painted against the supernatural, and "people did not call them hex signs until the 20th century". In 1924 Wallace Nutting heard of the hexenfoos ("witch's foot"), small protective marks etched on barn doors, often goose-foot shaped, and wrongly gave that meaning to the stars. Tourist hex signs boomed in the 1950s.

## 3. The Seal of Saturn and other house charms

The Seal of Saturn comes from learned planetary magic. Agrippa's "Three Books of Occult Philosophy" (Book II, chapter 22; complete edition 1533) gives seven "sacred tables of the planets". Saturn's is "a square of three": 1 to 9, every line and diagonal "making fifteen", the whole forty-five; rows 4 9 2, 3 5 7, 8 1 6. Divine names match its numbers, with Agiel its Intelligence and Zazel its spirit, and "out of the same numbers is drawn the seal, or Character of Saturn". Engraved on lead under a fortunate Saturn it helps childbirth, makes a man "safe, and powerfull" and wins petitions to princes; under an unfortunate Saturn it "hinders buildings, plantings". So the old record gives it safety, not house-warding; use as a house ward is modern. The seal's exact lines were not confirmed, so it is not drawn.

## 4. The pentagram as a door charm: the Drudenfuss

The Drudenfuss, "Drude's foot", is a pentagram or sometimes a hexagram. The Drude is a malevolent female night spirit of Germanic folklore, blamed for nightmares and said to press on sleepers' chests. The sign was painted or applied on house walls, cowshed and stable doors, church entrances, cradles and bedsteads; a fir-wood one in the cradle kept her off the infant. A horseshoe on the stable door and a holed stone by the cradle were other defences. In Goethe's "Faust" (1808) a pentagram on the threshold keeps Mephistopheles from leaving; he got in only because one outward angle was "left a little open". Literature, but it carries the belief that the figure must be whole and closed.

Drawing, faithful (one unbroken line, point up, closed back to the start):

- "M 50 8 L 25.31 83.98 L 89.94 37.02 L 10.06 37.02 L 74.69 83.98 Z"

## 5. The Sator square

A Latin word square reading the same four ways:

SATOR
AREPO
TENET
OPERA
ROTAS

Often read "the sower Arepo holds the wheels with care"; AREPO occurs nowhere else in Latin. The oldest are at Pompeii (house of Paquius Proculus, AD 50-79; a Palaestra column, before AD 62); others at Cirencester (found 1868) and Dura-Europos (about AD 200). The earliest begin ROTAS; SATOR first became usual in the early Middle Ages. In 1924-27 Christian Frank, Felix Grosser and Sigurd Agrell separately found the letters make PATER NOSTER twice, crossing at N, with A and O left over; its origin is unsettled. As a charm it went on discs thrown into fires to quench them and on bread against rabies. The Museum of Witchcraft and Magic records it used "for protection and for overcoming danger and difficulties", scratched on house walls or written on paper by cunning folk.

## 6. Scandinavian and Baltic folk signs

Norway: Vesterheim museum says symbols guarded barns, granaries and houses, and "most utilize opposites to confuse the evil forces": zigzags, motifs whirling opposite ways, S against Z. Zigzags guarded clothing openings; mara locks stood against the mare. Denmark: single or double mare-crosses were drawn or cut above a bed or on a cradle against the night-riding mare; a churn that would not make butter got an ember or heated horseshoe.

Iceland: the magical staves are sigils in grimoires from the 17th century on, not runes and not Viking: staves against witchcraft, thieves, drowning. The Vegvisir is first known from the Huld manuscript of 1860: carried, "one will never lose one's way in storms or bad weather". Legend, not record: that it is a Viking compass. The troll cross pendant is a late-1990s jewellery design by Kari Erlands of Dalarna, though older troll crosses were used in middle Sweden.

Latvia: Auseklis, the morning star, is an eight-line star traditionally drawn in one line. The National Trust records one at Belton, Lincolnshire, calling it one of the strongest signs against evil spirits. The thunder god's sign (a swastika form) was "carved on cribs and woven into the belts of newborns as protection against evil gazes"; its age is disputed.

## 7. Hamsa, nazar and other eye charms

The evil eye is harm done by a malicious stare; the charms stare it back or deflect it. Greek eye-cups (mostly 540-500 BC) carry large painted eyes, assumed evil-averting. The Turkish nazar (Greek mati) is a glass bead of concentric circles in dark blue, white, light blue and black. The hamsa ("five") is an open hand, often with an eye, used across North Africa and the Middle East by Jews (Hand of Miriam), Levantine Christians (Hand of Mary) and Muslims ("Hand of Fatima" is a colonial-era name); hands are painted on walls and doorways to protect mothers and newborns. Ancient roots are unproven. A hand cannot be drawn faithfully in a few strokes; the eye bead is the ring figure in section 1, coloured as above.

## 8. Ozark and Appalachian folk magic

Vance Randolph's "Ozark Superstitions" (1947; reissued as "Ozark Magic and Folklore", 1964) is the base record. As quoted secondhand (p. 287): "If the witch master knows the identity of the woman who is causing the trouble, he draws her picture on a board and fires a silver bullet into it." Appalachian protections are objects and acts, not carved signs: an iron horseshoe hung ends up, salt on windowsills and doorways, a broom bristles up so witches cannot ride it, blue porch ceilings against haints. No carved Ozark or Appalachian mark like the daisy wheel was found documented; none should be invented.

## 9. Hoodoo and the seals of the Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses

Hoodoo, or conjure and rootwork, is African-American folk magic. The "Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses" first circulated as cheap pamphlets in 18th-century Germany; Johann Scheible's 1849 Stuttgart printing made it widely known, and a New York English edition followed in 1880. Between 1936 and 1972 Harry Middleton Hyatt interviewed 1,600 African-American Christian root doctors and home practitioners, many of whom used it and similar seal-bearing books from mail-order suppliers. Its seals are stylised symbols ringed with pseudo-Hebrew and pseudo-Latin letters, each with an incantation and a note of its power. Seals were sold by the sheet; one practitioner describes them pinned inside a coat for protection, tucked into a mojo hand, or set beneath a candle. They belong to a specific book and practice and are not redrawn here.

## 10. Haitian Vodou veve

Veve are sacred drawings of Haitian Vodou, a living religion; most Haitians practise both Vodou and Catholicism. Veve summon the lwa (spirits): each lwa has its own, with regional variants, and the veve stands for the lwa during the ritual, offerings often placed on it. They are strewn on the peristil (temple) floor in cornmeal, ash, coffee grounds or powdered eggshell, usually symmetrically around the poto mitan, the centre post. The priest is an oungan, the priestess a manbo. In Rada ceremonies Papa Legba is saluted first; in Petwo rites usually Met Kalfou. Veve belong to their lwa and those who serve them; none is reproduced here, and a new mark should not borrow from them.

## How these were built

Rules from the record for designing a protective mark in the folk tradition:

1. Put it at the opening: door, window, hearth, threshold, stable door, bedstead, cradle, font, a dark crevice, a garment's edge. Harm comes in where the house or body is open.

2. Make the line hold. The daisy wheel is one endless figure; the Drudenfuss one closed stroke, since a gap let Mephistopheles in. Mesh gives a net or barrier. Rings catch an eye. A protective figure is closed, continuous, or a trap.

3. Use the tool of the place: compasses for petals, a chisel for straight cuts, a taper for burn marks, ink on paper for carried charms.

4. Call on a power by name or letter: VV, M, AM and PM call on the Virgin; Hohman's I. N. I. R. I. and Agrippa's tables carry holy names.

5. Use order and balance: the magic square sums to fifteen every way, the Sator square reads four ways, Norwegian makers set opposites to confuse evil.

6. Make it at the right time: Knole's marks went into green timber as the room was built; Agrippa's table must be made under a fortunate Saturn, or it harms.

7. Keep it plain and small: the Worcester daisy wheel is 4 cm across; many marks are crude.

8. Build from open sources. Building marks, geometry and letters are open to study; veve and hoodoo seals belong to living practices. Make a new mark from the principles, not by copying another people's sacred drawing.
