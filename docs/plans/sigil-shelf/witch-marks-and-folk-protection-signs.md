# Witch Marks and Folk Protection Signs

A study shelf, not a pattern book: what is recorded about marks made to keep harm out of houses, barns, churches and bodies. Drawings are SVG path strings in a 0-100 box, one string per stroke, in drawing order, labelled "faithful" (matches the recorded geometry) or "approximate" (recorded examples vary; this is one honest instance). What cannot be drawn faithfully is described in words. Legend is marked as legend.

## 1. Witch marks in English and European buildings

Historic England calls them ritual protection or apotropaic ("evil-turning") marks, usually scribed near doorways, windows and fireplaces to protect the people inside from witches and evil spirits. Timothy Easton, the Suffolk buildings historian who brought the subject to notice, records them clustering at doors, windows and chimney beams. In churches, compass-drawn designs are among the commonest graffiti found by the Norfolk Medieval Graffiti Survey (over 28,000 inscriptions in about half of Norfolk's 650 medieval churches since 2010); Matthew Champion notes many sit near the font. They belong with concealed shoes, witch bottles and horse skulls hidden at the same openings (Brian Hoggard, "Magical House Protection", 2019).

Caution: in July 2026 Jennifer Alexander (Warwick University) argued daisy wheels are masons' practice geometry and W and M shapes masons' ciphers. Some marks surely are; clustering at openings, and the burn marks, argue that others were made to protect.

### Daisy wheel (hexafoil)

The commonest mark: six petals inside a circle, drawn with compasses or any fixed-radius tool, the outer circle having the same radius as the arcs inside. At Tudor House, Worcester, one was laid out with compasses, then cut with a chisel. Sizes vary; unfinished ones have fewer arcs or are a bare circle. Historic England describes it as a single endless line that supposedly confused and trapped evil spirits. Legend, as told: demons follow a line to its end, so an endless line holds them.

### Marian marks: VV, M, AM, PM

Letters calling on the Virgin Mary. Conjoined V's, read as Virgo Virginum ("Virgin of Virgins"), make a W, or turned over an M for Maria. AM is read as Ave Maria; at Creswell Crags PM was read as Pace Maria. These are readings, not self-explaining inscriptions.

- "M 45 20 L 65 80 L 85 20"

### Mesh and ladder marks

Mesh marks are crossed lines cut like a net; Hoggard reads them as meant to catch the devil, noting they sit near entry points and are often too crude for carpenters' marks. Rising parallel lines or grid-iron shapes are called spirit ladders or Jacob's ladders; a bedroom ceiling at Woolpit, Suffolk, carried both. Easton reads them as traps for spirits; experts disagree.

### Rings, crosses, pentangles

Concentric rings are recorded as a daisy-wheel variant that catches a spirit in its rings, and as a "bull's eye" against the evil eye. A plain saltire (X) is also cited, as are crosses, criss-cross lines meant to confuse a following spirit, and pentangles (rare in Norfolk churches).

### Burn marks

Teardrop scorches on beams, door frames and panelling. Dean and Hill (Vernacular Architecture, 2014) burned candles, tapers and rushlights beside timber and found the teardrop practically impossible to make by accident; it comes from holding a flame steadily at about 45 degrees so its hottest part just touches the wood until it wears into a scoop. Easton suggests inoculation by sympathetic magic: a little burning keeps the great fire away. A burn mark is made by flame and time, not a path.

### Knole House, Kent

In 2014 MOLA archaeologists, working with the National Trust, found marks in the Upper King's Room, refitted for James I. Tree rings date the oak to early 1606, and the marks were cut while the wood was green, in the floor joists and around the fireplace: interlocking V's, criss-cross "demon traps", and scorch marks perhaps meant to protect against fire. The rooms were done by 1608; the king never came. The Gunpowder Plot link is a reading: Francis Young cautioned that joiners may have cut such marks whenever they laid a floor.

### Creswell Crags, Nottinghamshire and Derbyshire

In 2018 Hayley Clark and Ed Waters of Subterranea Britannica spotted marks on a cave tour; in February 2019 the site announced the largest concentration known in Britain, in the high hundreds in one cave. Forms include VV, PM, boxes and mazes.

### Historic England's survey

At Halloween 2016 Historic England asked the public to report marks; hundreds of records came in, many from historic houses.

## 2. Pennsylvania Dutch hex signs and braucherei

Braucherei, or powwow (an Algonquian loanword), is the Pennsylvania German healing and protective tradition brought by German-speaking immigrants in the 17th and 18th centuries: prayer, words and simple acts to heal people and livestock and guard against bewitchment. John George Hohman printed a Himmelsbrief ("heaven's letter") in 1802, to be hung in the house against disaster, and in 1820 "Der lange verborgene Freund" ("The Long Lost Friend"), the brauchers' handbook, which includes protection from bewitchment.

Barn stars are a separate thing that later merged with that reputation. Stars in circles appear on barns from the early 19th century, spreading as ready-mixed paint got cheap, peaking in the early 20th. Six-pointed stars and rosettes are traditional; a six-petalled rosette is the same compass figure as the daisy wheel. Patrick Donmoyer, who documented over 400 starred barns, says they were not painted against the supernatural, were seen as signs of divine order, were not called hex signs until the 20th century, and were never part of braucherei ritual. "Hexefuss" (witch's foot), recorded in 1924, meant the chicken-footprint mark a witch's presence left, a cross with two diagonal arms pointing up. The portable painted "hex sign" dates from about 1950 (Johnny Ott, from 1952, gave his discs magical meanings).

## 3. The Seal of Saturn and other house charms

The Seal of Saturn comes from learned planetary magic. The 3 by 3 magic square holds 1 to 9 so every row, column and diagonal sums to 15; Agrippa's "Three Books of Occult Philosophy" (1533, Book II, chapter 22) makes it the table of Saturn, rows 4 9 2, 3 5 7, 8 1 6 (Kircher prints a rotation, 6 1 8, 7 5 3, 2 9 4). From it come a seal and characters to engrave on a talisman, traditionally on lead, Saturn's metal; Saturn rules time and restriction and protects against evil. Agrippa's seal is a particular closed figure on the square; it is not drawn here because its exact lines were not confirmed. Use as a house ward is better documented in modern practice than in old record.

## 4. The pentagram as a door charm: the Drudenfuss

In German the pentagram is the Drudenfuss, "Drude's foot". The Drude is a night spirit or witch who sits on a sleeper's chest; the sign was drawn or carved on thresholds, doorways and bedposts to keep her out. Some accounts give a hexagram instead. In Goethe's "Faust" Part One, Mephistopheles cannot leave the study because of a pentagram on the threshold, and commentary explains that a break in it lets a spirit in but not out. That is literature, but it carries the belief that the figure must be whole and closed.

Drawing, faithful (one unbroken line, point up, closed back to the start):

## 5. The Sator square

A Latin word square reading the same four ways:

SATOR
AREPO
TENET
OPERA
ROTAS

Often read "the sower Arepo holds the wheels with effort"; AREPO occurs nowhere else and may be a name. The oldest are scratched at Pompeii, before 79 AD; others come from Cirencester (found 1868) and Dura-Europos. In 1926 Felix Grosser showed the letters rearrange into PATER NOSTER twice, crossing at N, with A and O left over; its origin is still argued. The Museum of Witchcraft and Magic records it used for protection and overcoming danger, scratched on house walls or written on paper by cunning folk.

## 6. Scandinavian and Baltic folk signs

Norway: the Vesterheim museum describes symbols guarding barns, storehouses and houses, most working by opposites to confuse evil, such as zigzags and whirls turning against each other. Denmark: single or double mare-crosses were drawn or cut over beds and cradles against the night-riding mare; a bewitched churn got an ember or hot horseshoe, fire and iron together.

Iceland: the magical staves are drawn seals from grimoires of about 1500 to 1860, not runes and not Viking. The Vegvisir is first known from the Huld manuscript of 1860; whoever carries it will not lose the way in storms. Legend, not record: that it is a Viking compass. The troll cross pendant is a late-1990s jewellery design by Kari Erlands of Dalarna, not folk.

Latvia: Auseklis, the morning star of interlaced lines, is called the strongest cross against the lietuvens, a nightmare spirit. The Perkons or fire cross, a swastika form, was carved on cribs and woven into newborns' belts against evil looks. Popular datings conflict.

## 7. Hamsa, nazar and other eye charms

The evil eye is harm done by an envious look; the charms stare it back or catch it. Greek "eye cups" of the 6th century BC, with great painted eyes, are among the earliest. The Greek mati and Turkish nazar are glass beads in rings: dark blue, white, light blue, dark centre. They hang on doors, boats and children. The hamsa is an open hand, often with an eye in the palm, used across North Africa and the Middle East by Jews (Hand of Miriam) and Muslims (Hand of Fatima); its ancient roots are claimed but thinly sourced. A hand cannot be drawn faithfully in a few strokes; the eye bead is the ring figure in section 1, coloured as above.

## 8. Ozark and Appalachian folk magic

Vance Randolph's "Ozark Superstitions" (1947; "Ozark Magic and Folklore", 1964) is the base record. His informants named witches, witch masters, power doctors and conjure folk; a witch master might draw the witch's picture on a board and shoot it with a silver bullet. Recorded Appalachian protections are objects and acts, not carved signs: a horseshoe over the door, a broom across the threshold, iron nails round the property, salt at the doorways. No carved Ozark or Appalachian mark like the daisy wheel was found documented; none should be invented.

## 9. Hoodoo and the seals of the Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses

Hoodoo, or conjure and rootwork, is African-American folk magic, practised mostly by Christians. The "Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses", an 18th or 19th-century German grimoire falsely ascribed to Moses, spread through an 1849 printing, and its seals became central in Black American use. From 1936 to 1972 Harry Middleton Hyatt interviewed about 1,600 root doctors and practitioners, many of whom used it and other seal-bearing books. By the 1920s the seals were sold as sheets to cut apart. A seal is copied onto brown paper, a petition written on the back, then carried in a mojo bag, set under a burning candle, or put in a jar spell. Each is a circle with an inner figure and Hebrew-like letters for one purpose. They belong to a specific book and practice and are not redrawn here.

## 10. Haitian Vodou veve

Veve are sacred drawings of Haitian Vodou, a living religion. Each lwa (spirit) has its own, varying by house and region; Britannica calls them geometric drawings that call down the energy of a particular lwa. An oungan (priest) or manbo (priestess) traces them freehand on the earthen temple floor, often at the foot of the poto mitan, the centre post, letting cornmeal or wood ash, or a mix, fall in a steady stream from between thumb and forefinger. Prayer, drumming and offerings bring it to life. Legba, keeper of the gate, is called first, his veve drawn at the start. A veve is not meant to last: dancing feet scatter it. Veve belong to their lwa and those who serve them; none is reproduced here, and a new mark should not borrow from them.

## How these were built

Rules from the record for designing a protective mark in the folk tradition:

1. Put it at the opening: door, window, hearth, chimney beam, threshold, bedpost, cradle, cave mouth. Harm comes in where the house is open; the mark goes where it would cross.

2. Make the line hold. The daisy wheel is one endless figure; the Drudenfuss is one closed stroke, since a gap lets a spirit in but not out. Mesh and ladder give a spirit lines to follow or a net to fall in. Rings catch an eye. A protective figure is closed, continuous, or a trap.

3. Use the tool of the place: compasses for circles and petals, a knife for straight cuts, a steady flame for burn marks, pen on brown paper for carried seals.

4. Call on a power by name or letter. VV, M, AM and PM call on the Virgin; the Sator square hides a prayer; seals carry divine names. Letters belong in a protective mark.

5. Use order and balance. The magic square and Sator square read the same every way; Norwegian carvers set opposite whirls against each other.

6. Make it once, at the right time, with intent. Knole's marks went into green timber as the room was built; a seal is copied, written on and consecrated before it is carried.

7. Keep it plain and small: most real marks are a hand-span or less, often crude. They are meant to work, not to be admired.

8. Build from open sources. Building marks, geometry and letters are open to study; veve and hoodoo seals belong to living practices. Make a new mark from the principles, not by copying another people's sacred drawing.
