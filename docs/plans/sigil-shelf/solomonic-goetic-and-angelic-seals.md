# Solomonic, Goetic and Angelic Seals: what the old books show and how they were built

Most of these seals hang on King Solomon, who in Jewish, Christian and Islamic legend commanded demons with a ring. None of the books is his; each is a later compilation borrowing his name.

Drawings are SVG path strings in a 0 to 100 box, one string per stroke, drawn in order. "Faithful" means the geometry is the documented figure. "Approximate": the parts are documented, the proportions are mine. What cannot be drawn faithfully, above all the 72 Goetic seals, is described in words.

## The legend underneath: Solomon's ring
In the Testament of Solomon, a Greek text dated between the first and fifth centuries AD, the archangel Michael brings Solomon a ring engraved with a seal the text calls a pentalpha, a five-pointed star. With it he binds the demon Ornias and sets demons to build the Temple. The Talmud (Gittin 68a) has Solomon, the demon king Asmodeus and a ring engraved with the divine name. Islamic legend makes it of brass and iron: brass to command good spirits, iron evil ones.

The six-pointed "Seal of Solomon" seems to arise in medieval Arabic tradition and pass into Spanish Kabbalah; an older Jewish origin has been proposed without positive evidence. The pentagram form looks like a Western Renaissance habit. The grimoires use the two stars interchangeably: the Heptameron's "Pentacle of Solomon" is drawn as a hexagram.

## The Key of Solomon (Clavicula Salomonis)
The Greater Key is probably fourteenth or fifteenth century and Italian, an estimate. Its ancestor is the Greek Hygromanteia (fifteenth century). Sloane 3847 is dated 1572. The usual English is S. L. MacGregor Mathers' 1889 edition. The French "Veritable Key" has more than twice as many pentacles as he printed.

Mathers prints 44 pentacles: seven each for Saturn, Jupiter, Mars and the Sun, five each for Venus and Mercury, six for the Moon. Book I chapter 18: make each on its planet's day and hour, on virgin parchment never used for anything else, or in the planet's metal purified by fire: lead for Saturn, tin for Jupiter, iron for Mars, gold for the Sun, copper for Venus, mixed metals for Mercury, silver for the Moon. Colours: black, celestial blue, red, gold or citron, green, mixed, silver, in the same order. Size is free. Another chapter says all on Wednesday in Mercury's hour.

The form is constant: a circle, a second circle inside it, and "in the space between the two thou shalt write the Names of God", or angel names, or a Hebrew verse from the Psalms. Inside sits the working figure: a square, cross, star, letter square, or rows of Hebrew letters that are names read across or down.

## The Lesser Key of Solomon and the Ars Goetia
The Lesser Key (Lemegeton) is a seventeenth-century English compilation in five parts: Ars Goetia, Theurgia Goetia, Ars Paulina, Ars Almadel, Ars Notoria. Its main manuscripts are British Library Sloane 3825 (seventeenth century, possibly 1641; Joseph Peterson's base text) and Sloane 2731, which lacks the Ars Notoria. Mathers' translation, published by Aleister Crowley in 1904, follows Sloane 3825 and itself warns that later copies are less reliable for the seals.

Johann Weyer printed 69 demons, the Pseudomonarchia daemonum, as an appendix to the 1577 edition of his De praestigiis daemonum, from a source he called the Liber officiorum spirituum. Weyer gives no seals. The Goetia, leaning on Reginald Scot's 1584 English version, changes the order, adds four spirits and drops Pruflas, making 72. Behind both stands the fifteenth-century French Livre des Esperitz, where 30 of 47 spirits match. The seals are the late, seventeenth-century layer.

Each entry gives rank, legions, the shape the spirit takes, its offices, and its seal, which "thou must wear as a Lamen upon thy breast". The "Observations" say the seals "are to be made in Metals": kings in gold, marquises in silver, dukes in copper, prelates in tin, knights in lead, presidents in Mercury, earls in copper and silver "alike equal." Mars and iron are missing (an annotated edition suggests iron for earls).

A representative set:
- Bael, the first: a king ruling in the East, 66 legions; three heads, toad, man and cat; makes one invisible. Gold.
- Paimon: a king, 200 legions, "very obedient unto Lucifer"; a man on a dromedary with "a crown most glorious" (the effeminate face is Weyer's). Mathers prints more than one version of his seal. Gold.
- Asmoday: a king under Amaymon, 72 legions; heads of bull, man and ram, a serpent's tail, a goose's webbed feet, flames from his mouth. Gold.
- Vassago: a prince of good nature, 26 legions; tells things past and to come, finds things lost and hidden.
- Buer: a president, 50 legions; teaches philosophy, logic and the virtues of herbs, and heals.
- Stolas: a prince, 26 legions; a mighty raven, then a man; teaches astronomy, herbs and precious stones.
- Andromalius, the seventy-second: an earl, 36 legions, holding a great serpent; brings back thieves and stolen goods.

The seals are not drawn here: each survives only as a picture, the copies differ, and estimated lines would make false seals. What they share:
- Each is set inside a ring (names lettered round the rim are a modern habit); inside are straight lines, crosses and arcs, usually near-symmetrical about an upright axis.
- Many strokes end in small circles. This is the oldest feature: the "ring letters" (charakteres, Brillenbuchstaben, "spectacle letters") of late antique Greek and Egyptian magic, commonly asterisks and straight lines with small circles at their ends and kept by Jewish magicians into the Middle Ages.
- Crescents appear as parts (the Secret Seal of Solomon has one with a flattened top); some strokes end in a short bar or hook instead of a circle.
- How each seal was derived is unknown. Claims of a Rose Cross cipher or the SATOR square behind them are modern and unproven.

Other instruments: a circle nine feet across with divine names along a coiled serpent; the Triangle of Art two feet outside it, apex toward the spirit's quarter, with Tetragrammaton, Primeumaton and Anaphaxeton on its sides and Michael split into Mi, Cha, El around its inner circle, where the spirit's seal goes; the Hexagram of Solomon on calfskin, worn on the skirt of the white robe and shown to make spirits take human shape; the Pentagram of Solomon in gold or silver, worn on the breast with the spirit's seal on its back; and the brass vessel, closed with the Secret Seal, in which Solomon shut the 72 and threw them in a lake near Babylon (legend).

## The Sigillum Dei and John Dee's Sigillum Dei Aemeth
The Seal of God first appears in the Liber Juratus, the Sworn Book of Honorius, in fourteenth-century manuscripts of a text perhaps late thirteenth century. The recipe: two concentric circles; at the top, the "southern angle", a cross whose foot slightly enters the inner circle; from the right of the cross the 72-letter name of God (Schemhamphoras) written round the band; in the centre a pentagram with the sign Tau in its middle, El above and Ely below. Heptagons and a heptagram with angel names fill the space between; modern accounts disagree on their count, so they are left out of the drawing.

In 1582 Dee, dissatisfied with the older versions, had angels dictate a new one through his scryer Edward Kelley (Mysteriorum Libri Quinti; drawing in Sloane MS 3188), named Aemeth (Hebrew emeth, "truth"). Its parts are two circles, a pentagram, heptagons and a heptagram. Secondary sources give 40 cells round the rim and seven seven-letter names in the heptagram's triangles; neither was checked against the manuscript. It was white wax, engraved both sides, about nine inches across. Uriel ordered a table two cubits square with the seal at its centre under the scrying stone, and a smaller wax seal under each leg.

## The Ars Notoria and its notae
The Notory Art of Solomon is a thirteenth-century grimoire for learning the liberal arts fast. Its notae are prayers in mixed Greek, Aramaic and Hebrew calling on angel names, and figures gazed at while praying. Scholars read the figures as divine symbols, vision devices or memory aids. None is drawn here.

## The Picatrix and the images of the planets
The Picatrix is the Arabic Ghayat al-Hakim, written in al-Andalus in the tenth or eleventh century and put into Castilian and Latin at Alfonso X's court around 1256. Its talisman is an image engraved at a chosen astrological moment to hold a planet's power; its seals are pictures, not linear marks. Book II chapter 10: Saturn, a man with a crow-like face and camel's feet on a throne, a spear in his right hand and a lance or dart in his left; Jupiter, per Apollonius, a man sitting on an eagle wrapped in a cloth; the Sun, a crowned king on a throne with a crow before him and the Sun's figure beneath his feet.

## Agrippa's planetary squares
Agrippa's Three Books of Occult Philosophy (1533), Book II chapter 22, gives "sacred tables of the planets", magic squares from three-by-three (Saturn) to nine-by-nine (the Moon), each with a divine name, an intelligence for good and a spirit for evil. Saturn's holds 1 to 9 with every line and diagonal making 15 and the whole 45. "From these same numbers are elicited a seal or character of Saturn and his spirit": lines drawn through the cells whose numbers spell the name, the method later used for new sigils.

The Golden Dawn rose cross lamen, a name traced as a path across 22 petals for the Hebrew letters, is a nineteenth-century invention in the same spirit.

## The Heptameron's circle
The Heptameron is doubtfully attributed to Peter de Abano (died about 1316); printed from the 1490s. Three circles, nine feet across, a hand's breadth apart. In the middle one: the name of the hour, the hour's angel and his sigil, the day's angel and his ministers, the season's name and spirits, the head of the sign, and the names of the earth, Sun and Moon for that season. In the outermost, at the four angles, the day's presiding angels of the air, a king and three ministers. In the innermost, four divine names with crosses between, Alpha toward the East, Omega toward the West, and a cross dividing the middle. Outside, at the four angles, pentagrams.

## How these were built
From what the books share, rules for designing a new seal:
1. Enclose it. Almost every seal, pentacle and circle sits in a ring; a double ring makes a band.
2. Fill the band with names: divine names, angel names or a verse, all the way round, starting at the top or at a cross marking the top.
3. Put one working figure in the centre: a star, cross, square, number square or letter grid. Choose its sides by number: five for Solomon's pentalpha, six for his hexagram, seven for the planets.
4. Let letters do the work: names in Hebrew, Greek or cipher placed at the figure's angles, read across a grid, or turned into a line joining the cells of a number square in the order the name runs.
5. Draw the mark in few strokes: lines, crosses, arcs and crescents, balanced about an upright axis, strokes ending in small circles, bars or hooks.
6. Tie it to one planet: its day and hour, metal (lead, tin, iron, gold, copper, mixed, silver) and colour (black, blue, red, gold, green, mixed, silver).
7. Make it on clean material: virgin parchment, or metal purified by fire.
8. One seal, one purpose: each pentacle has a single task, each Goetic seal a single named spirit.
9. Wear or set it: as a lamen on the breast, at the centre of a table, in the triangle, or under water, as its task needs.
