# Solomonic, Goetic and Angelic Seals: what the old books show and how they were built

Most of these seals hang on King Solomon, who in Jewish, Christian and Islamic legend commanded demons with a ring. None of the books is his; each is a later compilation borrowing his name.

## The legend underneath: Solomon's ring
In the Testament of Solomon, a Greek text dated anywhere from the late first century AD to the Middle Ages, the archangel Michael brings Solomon a ring with an engraved stone; the text calls the engraving a pentalpha, a five-pointed star. Thrown at the demon Ornias, the ring stamps and subdues him; Solomon then has the demons cut stone for the Temple. The Talmud (Gittin 68) has Solomon bind the demon Ashmedai (Asmodeus) with a ring engraved with the divine name. In medieval Islamic legend the ring was brass and iron, the two parts sealing written commands to good and evil spirits respectively.

The six-pointed "Seal of Solomon" seems to arise in medieval Arabic tradition and pass into Spanish Kabbalah; a pre-Islamic origin has been proposed (Scholem traced the names, not the design, to pre-Islamic Jewish magic). The pentagram form seems to arise in Western Renaissance magic. The grimoires blur the two: a seventeenth-century manuscript (Douce 116) calls a double circle with a hexagram inside a "pentacle of Solomon".

## The Key of Solomon (Clavicula Salomonis)
The Greater Key is probably a Latin or Italian text of the fourteenth or fifteenth century; Peter of Abano (died 1316) gives the earliest known reference to a Clavicula Salomonis. The oldest dated copy Peterson lists is Italian, 1446; the oldest Latin is late fifteenth century; a Greek "Magical Treatise of Solomon" with very similar contents survives in fifteenth-century copies. The usual English is S. L. MacGregor Mathers' 1889 edition, built almost entirely on eighteenth-century French manuscripts (Kings 288, Harley 3981, Sloane 3091). Peterson warns that the manuscripts do not number the pentacles, the oldest assign only some to planets, and many are late inventions drawn from Agrippa and others.

Mathers prints 44 pentacles: seven each for Saturn, Jupiter, Mars and the Sun, five each for Venus and Mercury, six for the Moon. Book I chapter 18: they strike terror into the spirits and reduce them to obedience. Usually they are engraved in the planet's metal in its days and hours, "and then there is no occasion to observe the rule of particular colours": lead for Saturn, tin for Jupiter, iron for Mars, gold for the Sun, copper for Venus, the mixture of metals for Mercury, silver for the Moon. Or they are written on exorcised virgin paper in the planet's colour: black, celestial blue, red, gold or citron, green, mixed colours, silver, in the same order. The material must be virgin, "never having been used for any other purpose", or metal purified by fire. Size is arbitrary "so long as they are made according to the rules." Book I chapter 8 adds: make them in Mercury's days and hours, Moon waxing, in gold, vermilion and azure.

The line "in the space between the two thou shalt write the names of God" belongs to chapter 8's consecration circle, not to the pentacles. The pentacles in Peterson's figures are a double ring whose band carries a verse, often with a small cross at the top. Inside sits the working figure: a square, cross, star, wheel, letter grid, or rows of Hebrew letters that are names read across or down.

## The Lesser Key of Solomon and the Ars Goetia
The Lemegeton ("The little Key of Solomon" in the manuscripts) is a seventeenth-century English compilation in five parts: Ars Goetia, Theurgia Goetia, Ars Paulina, Ars Almadel, Ars Notoria. Peterson's base text is Sloane 3825, the most accurate copy; the date 1641 occurs in its text and it uses books printed as late as 1671, so its present form is later. Sloane 2731 omits the Ars Notoria. Mathers' Goetia was published by Aleister Crowley in 1904; Mathers finds Dr. Rudd's codex full of errors "particularly in the Sigils."

Johann Weyer printed 69 demons, the Pseudomonarchia daemonum, as an appendix to the 1577 edition of his De praestigiis daemonum, from a source he called the Liber officiorum spirituum. He admits cutting passages "to render the whole work unusable", and his text mentions 72. Reginald Scot's 1584 English version lost Pruflas, the one Weyer spirit missing from the Goetia. The Goetia reorders the list and adds Vassago, Seere, Dantalion and Andromalius, making 72. The French Livre des Esperitz (fifteenth or sixteenth century) has 30 of its 47 spirits nearly identical to Goetic ones. The seals are the late layer: Peterson finds none in earlier manuscripts of the Offices of Spirits, though they resemble other catalogues, such as a fifteenth-century Florence manuscript with sigils for 100 demons.

Each entry gives rank, legions, the spirit's shape, its offices, and its seal or character, to be worn as a lamen. The "Observations" say the seals "are to be made in Mettalls": the chief kings in gold, marquises in silver, dukes in copper, prelates in tin, knights in lead, presidents in mercury, earls in copper and silver "equally alike." Mars and iron are missing; Mathers suggests iron for earls, and Peterson electrum for mercury.

A representative set, from Sloane 3825 unless marked:
- Bael, the first: a king ruling in the East, 66 legions; appears as a cat, a toad, a man, or all at once; speaks hoarsely; makes men invisible. Weyer gives him three heads, toad, man and cat; the Livre des Esperitz gives six legions. Gold.
- Paimon, the ninth: a great king, "very Obedient to Lucifer", 200 legions; a man on a dromedary with a glorious crown, trumpets and cymbals before him; observed toward the northwest. Weyer adds an effeminate countenance. Mathers prints two versions of his seal. Gold.
- Asmoday, the thirty-second: a king, 72 legions; three heads, bull, man and ram, a serpent's tail, flames from his mouth, goose feet, riding a dragon; seal worn as a lamen on the breast. Gold.
- Vassago, the third: a prince of good nature, 26 legions; declares things past and to come, finds things hidden or lost. Not in Weyer. Tin.
- Buer, the tenth: a president, 50 legions; teaches philosophy, logic and the virtues of all herbs and plants, heals all distempers. Mercury.
- Stolas, the thirty-sixth: a prince, 26 legions; a night raven, then a man; teaches astronomy and the virtues of herbs and precious stones. Tin.
- Andromalius, the seventy-second: an earl, a man holding a serpent; brings back thieves and stolen goods, finds hidden treasure. Not in Weyer. Copper and silver.

The seals are not drawn here: the copies differ, and estimated lines would make false seals. What they share (Peterson's manuscript figures, Mathers' printing):
- In Sloane 3825 the seals stand bare; the double ring with the spirit's name lettered round it is Mathers' 1904 presentation.
- Inside: straight lines, crosses, arcs, loops and curls. Some are symmetrical about an upright axis (Paimon's: four looped arches over a bar), others not (Bael's).
- Many strokes end in small circles, others in short cross-bars. Small end-circles mark the much older charakteres, "ring-letters" of Greek magic: asterisks and straight lines with small circles at their ends. Their medieval Jewish use (Bohak, 2011) was not read here.
- Mathers says the crescent in the Secret Seal of Solomon has a flattened top, "more like a bowl", laid across the figure.
- No source explains how any Goetic seal was derived.

Other instruments. The circle is nine feet across, divine names written round it from Eheie to Levanah; Mathers draws them along a coiled serpent but says the serpent is in only one private codex. The Triangle stands two feet from the circle, three feet across, toward the spirit's quarter, base nearest the circle (Mathers); Anephezeton, Tetragrammaton and Primeumaton run outside its sides, Mi-cha-el inside, round a central circle. The Hexagram, on calfskin, is worn at the skirt of the white vestment and shown to make the spirits take human shape. The Pentagram, in gold or silver, is worn on the breast with the spirit's seal on the other side. The Secret Seal, made at midnight on a Tuesday or Saturday in the blood of a black cock on virgin parchment, sealed the brass vessel in which Solomon shut the spirits before casting it into a lake in Babylon (legend; Weyer has a glass vessel).

## The Sigillum Dei and John Dee's Sigillum Dei Aemeth
The Seal of God first appears in the Liber Juratus, the Sworn Book of Honorius. The text may be late thirteenth century but probably not before 1316; the oldest copies are fourteenth or early fifteenth century. The recipe, in Peterson's translation: a circle "generally five fingers" across; a second circle inside it two barley grains away; in the uppermost part, "which is called the southern angle", a cross whose leg slightly enters the innermost circle; from the right of the cross the 72 letters of the name Schemhamphoras, evenly spaced round the band. In the centre a pentagram with the sign Tau in its middle, El above and Ely below. Round it a heptagon carrying the angels Casziel, Satquiel, Samael, Raphael, Anael, Michael and Gabriel; then a second heptagon whose sides cross (a heptagram); then a third whose angles touch the second's.

John Dee received what he took as the authoritative seal in 1582 through his scryer Edward Kelley; in his Mysteriorum Libri Quinti (sessions 1581 to 1583, Sloane MS 3188) it is called Aemeth or Emeth, "truth". Secondary accounts, not checked against the manuscript: the rim is divided into forty parts holding letters and numbers; inside come a heptagon of 49 cells lettered from a square of seven angels' names, a heptagram, two smaller heptagons, and the pentagram; it is wax, about nine inches across, with a cross and AGLA on the back. Peterson, from Dee's own summary: the seal sits in the middle of a sweet-wood table two cubits square, the scrying stone on top, and each leg stands on a smaller wax seal.

## The Ars Notoria and its notae
The Notory Art of Solomon is a thirteenth-century Latin text (its editor Julien Veronese says late twelfth or early thirteenth century, northern Italy) promising a sharpened mind and mastery of the arts. Its notae are cryptic prayers in mixed Greek, Aramaic and Hebrew together with diagrams (some scholars keep the word for the diagrams alone). Veronese links the figures to Neoplatonic divine symbols or vision devices; Yates, Page and Castle read them as memory aids. None is drawn here.

## The Picatrix and the images of the planets
The Picatrix is the Latin form of the Arabic Ghayat al-Hakim. Its author says he finished it in 348 AH (about 959); most scholars date it to the mid-eleventh century. Alfonso X of Castile ordered the Spanish translation between 1256 and 1258; the Latin came later, from the Spanish. Its planetary seals are pictures, not linear marks. Book II chapter 10: Saturn, "according to the opinion of the sage Picatrix", is a man with the face of a crow or stag and camel's feet, on a throne, a spear in his right hand and a lance or dart in his left. Jupiter, per Apollonius, is a man sitting on an eagle, wrapped in a cloth. It shaped Western magic from Marsilio Ficino onward.

## Agrippa's planetary squares
Agrippa's Three Books of Occult Philosophy (drafted 1510, complete edition Cologne 1533; English 1651), Book II chapter 22, gives "the sacred tables of the planets", magic squares from three-by-three (Saturn) to nine-by-nine (the Moon), each with divine names, "an Intelligency to what is good, with a spirit to what is bad." Saturn's holds 1 to 9, every line and diagonal making 15, the whole 45; its intelligence is Agiel and its spirit Zazel. Engraved on lead under a fortunate Saturn it helps childbirth and petitions. "Out of the same numbers is drawn the seal, or Character of Saturn, and of the spirits thereof", but Agrippa leaves the method to "the wise searcher"; the usual explanation, lines joining the cells whose numbers spell the name, is not in his text.

## The Heptameron's circle
The Heptameron's attribution to Peter of Abano (1250 to 1316) "seems quite certainly spurious" (Thorndike); the earliest printed edition Peterson has found is 1559. Three circles nine feet across, a hand's breadth apart. In the middle one: the name of the hour, the hour's angel and his sigil, the day's angel and his ministers, the season's name, its ruling spirits and presidents, the head of the sign, and the names of the earth, Sun and Moon for that season. In the outermost, at the four angles, the day's angels of the air, a king and three ministers. In the inner one, four divine names with crosses between (an older manuscript: Tetragrammaton, Adonay, Eloi, Agla), Alpha toward the East, Omega toward the West, and a cross dividing the middle. Outside, at the four angles, "Pentagones". Its garment pentacle, on kid-skin parchment, Peterson compares to the Goetia's six-angled figure.

## How these were built
From what the books share, rules for designing a new seal:
1. Enclose it. Pentacles, the Sigillum Dei and the circles sit in a double ring that makes a band. A bare spirit-mark, as in the Goetic manuscript, is the other choice.
2. Fill the band with names: divine names, angel names or a verse, all the way round, starting at the top or at a cross marking the top.
3. Put one working figure in the centre: a star, cross, square, number square or letter grid. Choose its sides by number: five for Solomon's pentalpha, six for his hexagram, seven for the planets.
4. Let letters do the work: names in Hebrew, Greek or cipher placed at the figure's angles or along its sides, or read across a grid. Joining the cells of a number square in the order the name runs is the later explanation of Agrippa's characters, not his stated rule.
5. Draw the mark in few strokes: lines, crosses, arcs, loops, curls and crescents, often balanced about an upright axis, strokes ending in small circles or short bars.
6. Tie it to one planet: its day and hour, metal (lead, tin, iron, gold, copper, mixed, silver) and colour (black, blue, red, gold, green, mixed, silver).
7. Make it on clean material: virgin parchment, or metal purified by fire.
8. One seal, one purpose: each pentacle has a single task, each Goetic seal a single named spirit.
9. Wear or set it: as a lamen on the breast, on the back of a breast-pentagram, at the centre of a table, sewn on a garment, or under water, as its task needs.
