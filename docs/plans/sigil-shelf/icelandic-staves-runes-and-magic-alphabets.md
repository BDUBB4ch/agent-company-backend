# Icelandic Staves, Runes and Magic Alphabets

Study material, not a pattern book: what the Icelandic staves, the runes and the Renaissance magic alphabets were, where each is first found, and how each was built. Simple, well documented forms have a drawing (0-100 box, y grows downward, one string per stroke, in order), labelled faithful or approximate; the rest are described in words only.

## The sources

Galdrabók means book of magic and is the Icelandic word for any magic book; "the" Galdrabók is the best known one. It is a pocket manuscript now in Stockholm, written by several hands, Icelanders from the later 1500s into the 1600s and a last scribe who may have been a Dane copying Icelandic material; the usual dating is about 1550 to 1650. J. G. Sparfvenfelt acquired it in 1682. Stephen Flowers translated it in 1989. The Swedish History Museum holds two Icelandic books of black magic, one parchment and one paper, written late 1500s to mid-1600s; their spells call on Jesus, God and the Trinity beside Odin, and Kaupaloki is drawn in one.

The Huld manuscript (ÍB 383 4to, National Library of Iceland) was compiled by Geir Vigfússon (1813-1880) in Akureyri, in 1860 by most accounts (the Museum of Icelandic Sorcery and Witchcraft says 1847). Huld means secrecy. Its main body is about 300 alphabets, secret letters and runes; a later section holds about 30 staves with texts. Vigfússon copied from at least three older manuscripts. The modern drawn forms of Vegvísir and Ægishjálmur come from it.

The Lbs manuscripts are the National Library's own. Lbs 143 8vo is a Galdrakver (little magic book) of 27 parchment leaves, one unknown hand, 1670, once owned by Hannes Finnsson, Bishop of Skálholt: hymns, then charms, prayers and staves with instructions, with an Ægishjálmur on f. 10v. A 19th-century library galdrabók has a Vegvísir and a stave for knowledge to be carved on lignite.

AM 434a 12mo, a small medical book, is cited for the earliest sign resembling the Ægishjálmur and for Gapaldur and Ginfaxi. Its date is disputed: one compendium says about 1490-1510, the Wikipedia list says 17th century.

The Museum of Icelandic Sorcery and Witchcraft opened in Hólmavík in the Westfjords in June 2000, run by Strandagaldur. Strandir is where sorcery is said to have taken the deepest root. Jón Rögnvaldsson was burned in Eyjafjörður in 1625, the first burning for sorcery in Iceland, after pages of runic characters and signs were found among his things; three men were burned at Trékyllisvík in Strandir in 1654; about 170 trials are recorded.

On dating: no Icelandic stave can be traced to the Viking Age. The surviving books run from about 1500 (if AM 434a's early date holds) to the late 1800s, most from the 17th century on, mixing learned Solomonic seal-magic from Latin Europe, Christian prayers and names, and older Norse words and runes. They are not runes, though some contain runic or cipher letters.

## The named staves

### Ægishjálmur (Helm of Awe, Helm of Terror)
The name is old; the drawing is not. In the Eddic poem Fáfnismál the dragon Fáfnir says he wore an ægishjálmr to frighten people while he lay on his gold, and Sigurd answers that it is no shelter against a brave man; the poem never says what it looked like. The drawn sign is centuries later. In AM 434a 12mo a sign like it is drawn on the forehead with yarrow soaked in one's own blood to calm a chieftain's anger. Lbs 143 8vo (1670): make it in lead and press it to the forehead before meeting enemies. Jón Árnason's 19th-century folklore collection: make it in lead, press the lead between the eyebrows and say "Ægishjálm er ég ber milli brúna mér" (I bear the helm of awe between my brows), and a man can meet his enemies sure of victory. The Galdrabók's 41st spell draws one with four arms and none of the cross-strokes.

Construction in words: a small circle or point at the centre; four or eight identical straight arms at equal angles; each arm ends in a trident (a short bar across the tip, its ends bent outward, the arm running on as the middle prong); eight-arm forms add one or more short bars across each arm partway out. Identical arms and rotational symmetry define the type.

### Vegvísir (Way-shower)
Carried so you will not lose your way in storms or bad weather. Huld: "Beri maður stafi þessa á sér villist maður ekki í hríðum né vondu veðri þó ókunnugur sé." Honest dating: it has a single line of descent. It is first found in Huld (folio 27r), and the only other copies, a Galdrakver by Olgeir Geirsson (Akureyri, 1868-1869) and an anonymous Eyjafjörður book, are later books from the same district. There is no earlier drawing and no runestone or Viking object; the name "Viking compass" is modern, and claims it was drawn in blood on the forehead trace to no manuscript.

Construction in words: a central point or small circle with eight equal straight arms at equal angles, like a compass rose, but every arm ends differently: forks or tridents, crossbars, small rings, hooks, half-circles, dots, sometimes several on one arm. No drawing: the exact terminals in Huld could not be checked, and a guessed set would be invention.

### Gapaldur and Ginfaxi
A pair for winning at glíma, Icelandic wrestling: Gapaldur in the shoe under the right heel, Ginfaxi under the toes of the left foot; some accounts say they work only together. The museum cites AM 434a 12mo; most modern pages repeat one shared description. Construction in words: compact radial staves of straight strokes with hooks and crossbars, small enough for a shoe. No drawing.

### Lukkustafir (Luck staves)
Carried for good luck; one list adds protection on journeys. A similar sign, Heillahnútur (luck-knot), has about the same purpose in the manuscripts. No drawing.

### Draumstafir (Dream staves)
To dream what your heart desires. The museum: inscribe these characters on silver or a white hide on Midsummer's Night and sleep on them. A different sign, a "sigil of Solomon," is carved on lignite and kept under the head for good dreams and wisdom. Construction in words: a row or cluster of characters rather than one radial sign, slept upon. No drawing.

### Smjörhnútur (Butter knot)
A test drawn on butter. In folklore a witch made a tilberi, a creature that sucked milk from other people's animals; butter from that milk was soft and tasted strange, and when the butter knot was drawn on it, it curdled, foamed away or vanished. A very similar stave appears in Huld as Óttastafur (fear stave). No drawing.

### Kaupaloki
For luck and success in trade, drawn in one of the Swedish History Museum's books. One modern guide says it was drawn on old paper and hidden under the left arm; that detail could not be traced to a manuscript. No drawing.

### Angurgapi
Inscribed on a pot lid or the bottom of a tub, says the museum, which calls it one of the most powerful staves. The often-repeated purpose, stopping barrels leaking, has no reference behind it; older versions of the list linked it to harmful work. Its purpose is not securely recorded. No drawing.

### Brýnslustafir (Whetstone staves)
For use on whetstones. No source read describes the form or the working. No drawing.

### Others in the same books
Dreprún, to kill an enemy's cattle. A stave for when evil approaches, carved in oak and painted with blood from the user's hand. One 19th-century instruction: "Draw blood from above your left middle finger nail and with it write this stave on a piece of paper."

## The runes

### Elder Futhark
Twenty-four runes, from the first inscriptions around the 1st century CE to about the 8th century, named for its first letters f u þ a r k and split into three groups of eight (ættir). The Kylver stone on Gotland (about 400 CE) has the earliest full row. Sounds in order: f u þ a r k g w; h n i j ï p z s; t b e m l ŋ d o. The names are Proto-Germanic reconstructions from the medieval rune poems and Gothic letter names, some secure (*fehu, cattle or wealth; *raidō, ride), some disputed (*ūruz, aurochs or water): *þurisaz giant, *ansuz god, *kaunan, *gebō gift, *wunjō joy, *hagalaz hail, *naudiz need, *īsaz ice, *jēra year, *eihwaz yew, *perþ- unknown, *algiz, *sōwilō sun, *tīwaz the god Tyr, *berkanan birch, *ehwaz horse, *mannaz man, *laguz water, *ingwaz, *dagaz day, *ōþala inheritance.

Construction: one or two upright staves with branches at an angle, almost no curves, few horizontals; the angular shapes are taken to suit cutting wood or metal. Carved examples vary; the drawings are the standard normalised forms.

### Younger Futhark
By the end of the 8th century Scandinavia had cut the row to sixteen runes, one rune serving several sounds. Names from the Icelandic and Norwegian rune poems: fé (wealth), úr (rain or iron), þurs (giant), óss (god), reið (ride), kaun (ulcer); hagall (hail), nauðr (need), íss (ice), ár (plenty), sól (sun); Týr, bjarkan (birch), maðr (man), lǫgr (sea), ýr (yew). Long-branch runes appear mostly on stone, short-twig runes in everyday messages on wood; nine short-twig runes are simplified long-branch forms and seven are identical.

### Bind runes (bandrúnir)
Two, or rarely three, neighbouring runes joined into one sign, usually sharing one stave. A second kind, the same-stave rune, strings several runes along one long stemline, which can become part of a picture, such as a ship's mast or sail (Södermanland 158). Bind runes are common in Proto-Norse and medieval inscriptions and extremely rare in Viking Age ones. The standard early example is the Kragehul I spear shaft from Funen, Denmark, found in 1877 at a war-booty sacrifice site: its "gagaga" is three bind runes, each the X of g with the twigs of a attached to the ends of its arms, perhaps a chant or battle cry. No drawing of Kragehul: the twig placement could not be checked.

## The magic alphabets

### Theban (the "witches' alphabet")
First printed in Johannes Trithemius's Polygraphia (1518), a book of ciphers, credited to Honorius of Thebes on the word of Pietro d'Abano. That attribution is legend: it is in none of d'Abano's writings and in no manuscript of the Sworn Book of Honorius except one composite London manuscript (British Library Sloane 3853) that names Agrippa as its source. Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, Trithemius's pupil, printed it in De occulta philosophia, Book III, chapter 29 (1533). Its use as the witches' script of Books of Shadows is 20th-century Wiccan. Construction in words: a letter-for-letter substitute for Latin, 24 characters, one case, left to right; no J, U or W, written with the letters for I, V and VV. No drawings: the letter shapes could not be checked against Agrippa's chart.

### Malachim
Agrippa, same chapter. The 1651 English translation: "Malachim, or Melachim, i.e. of Angels, or Regal"; malachim is Hebrew for angels or messengers. A substitute for Hebrew on Hebrew and Greek letter shapes, 23 signs for 22 letters (samekh has two).

### Celestial alphabet
Agrippa, Book III, chapter 30: "the Hebrews have a writing which they call Celestiall, because they shew it placed and figured amongst the Stars." Twenty-two consonant characters named after Hebrew letters. It is not the Enochian of John Dee and Edward Kelley, sometimes also called celestial.

### Passing the River (Transitus Fluvii)
The third Hebrew-derived script Agrippa names, a substitute for the 22 Hebrew letters. The name is explained as the Jews crossing the Euphrates back from Babylon, or the river Chebar; both are guesses.

Construction of the three: thin straight strokes and simple angles, with small rings at the ends of many strokes. These are ring letters (German Brillenbuchstaben, spectacle letters), descended from the charaktêres of Graeco-Egyptian magic, whose mark is ringlets at the tips, and early modern Europe took Hebrew-like letters with ringed tips for angelic alphabets. Agrippa's account of the Celestial script, letters figured among the stars, gives a dot-and-line build: points as stars, straight lines between them. No drawings: single letters could not be checked against the printed tables.

## How these were built

The rules the old makers followed, taken from the forms above. A sign made by them belongs to the tradition without copying any one stave.

1. Pick the frame: radial (a centre point, cross or small circle with four or eight equal arms at equal angles, as in Ægishjálmur and Vegvísir) or stave (uprights carrying angled branches, as in runes).

2. Use straight strokes for anything carved. Runes avoid curves and most horizontals; staves are straight lines with small rings, hooks or half-circles at the ends.

3. End every arm on purpose. The terminals are few: trident, crossbar, double crossbar, ring, hook, half-circle, dot. The helm repeats one terminal on every arm; the way-shower gives each arm a different one. Short bars across an arm partway out are a separate element.

4. Bind letters into one sign: two or three runes on a shared stave, several along one stemline, or one letter's twigs hung on another's arms (Kragehul).

5. Hide the words. Huld gathered some 300 secret and runic alphabets; writing the purpose in a cipher alphabet and fusing the letters stays within the tradition.

6. Ring the tips, as the angelic scripts do.

7. Join stars: set points, then straight lines between them, so the sign reads as a constellation.

8. Size it to its place. The helm on the brow is fully symmetrical; staves for a shoe, a whetstone or a lid are compact.

9. Name the material, place and time: lead on the brow, blood and yarrow, oak painted with blood, lignite, silver or white hide on Midsummer's Night, under a heel, on butter, on a whetstone, on a pot lid.

10. Pair it with words. Most staves came with a short text or formula, and the books mixed Christian names (God, Jesus, AGLA) with Norse ones (Odin).

11. Expect variation. No stave had one fixed form; copies differ in arm count, bars and endings. A new sign may be a variant as much as an invention.
