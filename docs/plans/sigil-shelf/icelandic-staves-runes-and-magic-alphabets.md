# Icelandic Staves, Runes and Magic Alphabets

Study material, not a pattern book: what the Icelandic staves, the runes and the Renaissance magic alphabets were, where each is first found, and how each was built. Simple, well documented forms have a drawing (0-100 box, y grows downward, one string per stroke, in order), labelled faithful or approximate; the rest are described in words only.

## The sources

Galdrabók means book of magic and is the Icelandic word for any magic book; "the" Galdrabók is the best known one, and that name for it dates from 1926 at the earliest. It is a small manuscript in Stockholm with 47 numbered spells and staves, written by four hands over about a century (usual dating about 1550 to 1650, or "c. 1600"): three Icelanders (spells 1-10 in the later 1500s, 11-39, then 40-44 in a 17th-century hand) and a fourth, a Dane, who copied Icelandic material (end of 44 to 47). Stephen Flowers translated it into English in 1989. The Swedish History Museum holds two pocket-sized Icelandic books of black magic, one parchment and one paper, each by at least two hands, late 1500s to mid-1600s; their spells call on Jesus, God and the Trinity beside Odin. The museum says the Swedish antiquarian Johan Gabriel Sparfwenfeld bought the book in Copenhagen in 1682. It names Ægishjálmur, Vatnahlífir (safety from water) and Kaupaloki.

The Huld manuscript (ÍB 383 4to, National Library of Iceland) was compiled by Geir Vigfússon (1813-1880) in Akureyri, in 1860 by most accounts (the Museum of Icelandic Sorcery and Witchcraft says 1847). It has 27 paper leaves. It opens with about 300 alphabets, secret letters and rune rows; its second part holds 30 staves with texts. Vigfússon copied from at least three older manuscripts, one apparently written at Seltjarnarnes around 1810. The oldest known Vegvísir is in it.

The Lbs manuscripts are the National Library's own. Lbs 143 8vo is a Galdrakver (little magic book) of 27 vellum leaves, one unknown hand, 1670; one compendium says it was owned by Hannes Finnsson, Bishop of Skálholt, but gives no source for that. Leaves 1-7 are hymns; the rest are charms, prayers and staves with instructions, with an Ægishjálmur on f. 10v; the museum says most of its staves are for protection. Lbs 4627 8vo, a 19th-century Eyjafjörður galdrabók, has a Vegvísir and a stave for gaining knowledge to be carved on lignite. Lbs 2413 8vo (about 1800) has nine Helms of Awe, each to be used 99 times.

AM 434a 12mo, a small medical book (Lækningakver) in the Arnamagnæan Collection, is cited for the earliest drawn sign resembling the Ægishjálmur, a stave against the wrath of chieftains (Við höfðingja reiði) and a stave against thieves (Þjófastafur). Its date is disputed: one compendium says about 1500 (1490-1510); the Wikipedia list says 17th century.

The Museum of Icelandic Sorcery and Witchcraft opened in Hólmavík in June 2000, run by Strandagaldur. Jón Rögnvaldsson, one of the first executed for sorcery in Iceland, was burned in Eyjafjörður in 1625; the contemporary annal says he raised a dead man against a boy, and the papers with runes found among his things are reported only about two centuries later. Three men were burned at Trékyllisvík in 1654. Wikipedia counts 120 witch trials from 1625 to 1686; a 2025 review of the edited verdicts counts 22 burnings (21 men, one woman).

On dating: no Icelandic stave can be traced to the Viking Age. The surviving books run from about 1500 (if AM 434a's early date holds) to about 1900 (the priest Jónas Jónasson, d. 1918, compiled one titled Galdrastafir), most from the 17th century on, mixing learned Solomonic seal-magic from Latin Europe, Christian prayers and names, and older Norse words and runes. They are not runes, though some contain runic or cipher letters.

## The named staves

### Ægishjálmur (Helm of Awe, Helm of Terror)
The name is old; the drawing is not. In the Eddic poem Fáfnismál (stanzas 16-17) the dragon Fáfnir says he wore the ægishjálmr to frighten people, and Sigurd answers that it shields no one against a brave man; the poem never says what it looked like. The drawn sign is centuries later, inspired by Latin seal-magic such as the Clavis Salomonis. In AM 434a 12mo a sign like it is drawn on the forehead with yarrow drenched in one's own blood, then one goes before one's master invoking names including AGLA and the Ophanim, to stem a chieftain's anger. Lbs 143 8vo (1670): make it in lead and press it to the forehead when you expect to meet enemies. Jón Árnason's 19th-century folklore collection: make it in lead, press the lead between the eyebrows and say "Ægishjálm er ég ber / milli brúna mér!" (I bear the helm of awe between my brows), and "thus a man could meet his enemies and be sure of victory." The Galdrabók's 41st spell draws one with only four arms and without the lines across the arms. Names and shapes were never standardised; one early 19th-century book (JS 375 8vo) calls one version "the Seal of Moses."

Construction in words: a central point or small circle; four or eight identical straight arms at equal angles, in the common form "eight arms that look like spiked tridents"; in the simplest form it looks like four M- or Z-runes; fuller forms add short lines across each arm partway out. Identical arms and rotational symmetry define the type.

### Vegvísir (Way-shower)
Carried so you will not lose your way in storms or bad weather. Huld: "Beri maður stafi þessa á sér villist maður ekki í hríðum né vondu veðri þó ókunnugur sé" (carry this sign with you and you will not get lost in storms or bad weather, even in unfamiliar surroundings). Honest dating: it has a single line of descent. It is first found in Huld (page 60, folio 27r). The only other copies are Lbs 2917 a 4to, a Galdrakver by Olgeir Geirsson, Akureyri, 1868-1869, and Lbs 4627 8vo, an anonymous 19th-century Eyjafjörður book (f. 17v), whose text makes it work "if you believe in God in the name of Jesus." The three books copy one another. There is no earlier drawing and no runestone, Viking object, Edda or saga; "Viking compass" is recent popular writing. The story that seafarers painted it on their foreheads with coal traces to the singer Björk, not to any manuscript.

Construction in words: the manuscript versions are squarish and carry no runes; the circular forms ringed with a rune row are 20th-century. The common drawing has eight arms at equal angles from a centre, like a compass rose, each ending differently; that description is unconfirmed against the Huld page itself. No drawing: the exact terminals in Huld could not be checked, and a guessed set would be invention.

### Gapaldur and Ginfaxi
A pair for winning at glíma, Icelandic wrestling: two staves kept in the shoes, Gapaldur under the heel of the right foot and Ginfaxi under the toes of the left foot; one modern guide says they work only together. Their source manuscript is unconfirmed, and their form was not checked. No drawing.

### Lukkustafir (Luck staves)
From Huld (ÍB 383 4to): "Whoever carries this symbol with them encounters no evil, neither on the sea nor on the land." Unconfirmed (search extracts only): a related Heillahnútur (luck-knot), said to be in Lbs 2413 (about 1800), "should always be carried on one's body." No drawing.

### Draumstafir (Dream staves)
To dream what your heart desires; the Wikipedia list cites Huld. The museum: inscribe these characters "on silver or a white hide on Midsummer's Night," sleep on them, and you will dream what you wish. A different sign, the Sigil of Solomon, is carved on lignite and kept under your head to be wise and have good dreams. No drawing.

### Smjörhnútur (Butter knot)
A test drawn on butter. In folklore only women could make a tilberi, from a rib taken from a fresh grave; it sucked milk from other people's cows and ewes and vomited it into its owner's churn. Butter from that milk "will clump together as if curdled, or even melt away into foam"; the sign of the cross over it, or the smjörhnútur drawn in it, exposes it. No drawing.

### Kaupaloki
For success in trade, drawn on the right-hand page of the Swedish History Museum's paper book. The museum in Hólmavík (stave titled "For trading"): draw it on "furry paper" and keep it secretly under your left arm. No drawing.

### Angurgapi
Inscribed on a pot lid or the bottom of a tub, says the museum, which calls it one of the most powerful staves. The often-repeated purpose, carved on barrel ends to stop leaks, is marked "citation needed" on Wikipedia. Its purpose is not securely recorded. No drawing.

### Brýnslustafir (Whetstone staves)
From Huld, for use on whetstones (sharpening knives). The museum: carve one stave on each side of the sharpening stone. The form was not checked. No drawing.

### Others in the same books
Dreprún, to kill an enemy's cattle (a 17th-century grimoire in the Antikvarisk-Topografiska Arkivet, Stockholm). Skelkunarstafur, to make your enemies afraid; a similar-looking stave is titled Óttastafur (fear stave) in Huld. The "When evil is approaching" staves: carve them on oak, paint them with blood from your hand and hang them over the door, and "little evil will then enter." The epigraph of the 2025 edition of Jónas Jónasson's Galdrastafir manuscript: "Draw blood from above your left middle finger nail and with it write this stave on a piece of paper."

## The runes

### Elder Futhark
Twenty-four runes, used from the 2nd to the 8th century, named for its first letters f u þ a r k and split into three groups of eight (ættir, a modern use of an Old Norse word for lineage; the original group names are unknown). The oldest dated inscription is the Vimose comb from Funen (about 160 CE). The Kylver stone on Gotland (about 400 CE) has the earliest listing of the row. Sounds in order: f u þ a r k g w; h n i j ï p z s; t b e m l ŋ d o. The names are Proto-Germanic reconstructions. Fairly secure: *fehu cattle, wealth; *ansuz god; *raidō ride, journey; *gebō gift; *wunjō joy; *hagalaz hail; *naudiz need; *īsaz ice; *jēra- year, harvest; *sōwilō sun; *tīwaz the god Tyr; *berkanan birch; *ehwaz horse; *mannaz man; *ingwaz Ing; *dagaz day; *ōþala- inheritance. Uncertain: *ūruz aurochs; *þurisaz giant; *kauną boil; *ī(h)waz yew; *perþō unknown; *algiz elk (widely thought not the original name; "protection" has been proposed); *laguz water.

Construction: one or two upright staves with branches at an angle, almost no curves, few horizontals; the angular shapes were presumably an adaptation to cutting wood or metal. Carved examples vary; the drawings are the standard normalised forms.

### Younger Futhark
By the late 8th century Scandinavia had cut the row to sixteen runes, one rune serving several sounds. Names from the Icelandic and Norwegian rune poems: fé (wealth), úr (rain), þurs (giant), áss (a god), reið (ride), kaun (ulcer); hagall (hail), nauðr (need), íss (ice), ár (plenty), sól (sun); Týr, bjǫrk (birch), maðr (man), lǫgr (sea), ýr (yew). Long-branch runes appear mostly on stone, short-twig runes in everyday messages on wood; nine short-twig runes are simplified long-branch forms and seven are identical.

### Bind runes (bandrúnir)
Two, or sometimes three, neighbouring runes joined into one sign by sharing a stroke. A second kind, the same-stave rune, strings several runes along one long stemline, which can become part of a picture: on Sö 158 at Ärsta, Södermanland, the stemline is a boat's mast. Bind runes are common in Proto-Norse and medieval inscriptions and extremely rare in Viking Age ones. The standard early example is the Kragehul I lance shaft (DR 196 U) from Funen, found in 1877 at a war-booty sacrifice site (deposits about 200-475 CE): its "gagaga" is three bind runes, each built on the X of g with short side-twigs attached to the ends of the X to make a, perhaps a chant or battle cry (that reading is marked "citation needed"). No drawing of Kragehul: the twig placement could not be checked.

## The magic alphabets

### Theban (the "witches' alphabet")
First printed in Johannes Trithemius's Polygraphia (1518), a book of ciphers, credited to Honorius of Thebes "as Pietro d'Abano testifies in his greater fourth book." No known writing by d'Abano mentions it, and of the Sworn Book of Honorius manuscripts only British Library Sloane 3853 has it, naming Agrippa as its source. Agrippa printed it in De occulta philosophia, Book III, chapter 29 (1533). Wicca later took it up for hiding magical writing. Construction in words: a letter-for-letter substitute for the old Latin alphabet, one case, left to right; J and U are written with I and V; in Trithemius's chart W comes after Z. No drawings: the shapes could not be checked against the printed chart.

### Malachim
Agrippa, Book III, chapter 30 (chapter and wording from search extracts of the 1651 English text, unconfirmed in full): "Malachim, or Melachim, i.e. of Angels, or Regal." Malachim is Hebrew for angels or messengers. Derived from Hebrew and Greek letters; 23 signs for 22 Hebrew letters, samekh having two. Mostly straight lines with circles or large dots at their ends; some look like constellations.

### Celestial alphabet
Agrippa, Book III (chapter 30 and the 1651 wording per the same unconfirmed extracts: "a writing which they call Celestiall, because they shew it placed and figured amongst the Stars"). Twenty-two characters named after Hebrew letters. Not the Enochian of John Dee and Edward Kelley, sometimes also called celestial.

### Passing the River (Transitus Fluvii)
The third Hebrew-derived script in Agrippa's Book III (printed 1533, written about 1510): 22 characters. Abraham de Balmis (1523), Geoffroy Tory (1529) and Panteo (1530) printed it before Agrippa. The name is explained as the Jews crossing the Euphrates back from Babylon, or the river Chebar; both are guesses.

Construction of the three: thin straight strokes and simple angles, with small rings or dots at the ends of many strokes. The charaktêres of Graeco-Egyptian magic, letter-like signs with no sound or meaning, used the same device: their commonest forms are asterisks and straight lines with small circles at the ends (ring letters, German Brillenbuchstaben). A line of descent to Agrippa's scripts is unconfirmed here. Reading the Celestial script as points joined like stars is an inference from Agrippa's words, not his instruction. No drawings: single letters could not be checked against the printed tables.

## How these were built

The rules the old makers followed, taken from the forms above. A sign made by them belongs to the tradition without copying any one stave.

1. Pick the frame: radial (a centre point, cross or small circle with four or eight equal arms at equal angles, as in Ægishjálmur and Vegvísir) or stave (uprights carrying angled branches, as in runes).

2. Use straight strokes for anything carved. Runes avoid curves and most horizontals; the helm is built of straight arms, tridents and cross-lines.

3. End every arm on purpose. In the common modern drawings the terminals are few: trident, crossbar, double crossbar, ring, hook, half-circle, dot. The helm repeats one terminal on every arm; the common way-shower drawing gives each arm a different one. Short bars across an arm partway out are a separate element.

4. Bind letters into one sign: two or three runes on a shared stave, several along one stemline, or one letter's twigs hung on another's arms (Kragehul).

5. Hide the words. Huld gathered some 300 secret and runic alphabets; writing the purpose in a cipher alphabet and fusing the letters stays within the tradition.

6. Ring the tips, as the angelic scripts do.

7. Join stars: set points, then straight lines between them, so the sign reads as a constellation (an inference from Agrippa's words and the look of the Malachim, not a recorded rule).

8. Size it to its place. The helm pressed on the brow is fully symmetrical; a stave for a shoe, a whetstone or a pot lid has to fit that surface.

9. Name the material, place and time: lead on the brow, blood and yarrow, oak painted with blood and hung over the door, lignite under the head, silver or white hide on Midsummer's Night, paper under the left arm, under a heel, on butter, on each side of a whetstone, on a pot lid.

10. Pair it with words. Most staves came with a short text or formula, and the books mixed Christian names (God, Jesus, AGLA) with Norse ones (Odin).

11. Expect variation. No stave had one fixed form; copies differ in arm count, bars and endings. A new sign may be a variant as much as an invention.
